## 1. His process, in order

He has stated the sequence in the same form since 2014. The five steps, from the post that introduced them:

> "Step 1: Develop a narrative for the business that you are valuing or considering investing in: Every business has a story line and the place to start a valuation is with that narrative. While managers and founders get to present their narrative first, and some of them are more persuasive and credible than others, you and I have to develop our own narratives, sometimes in sync with and sometimes at odds with the management story line."
>
> "Step 2: Test the narrative against history, experience and common sense: This is the stage at which you put your narrative through a reality test and examine whether it withstands multiple tests. The first is the test of history, where you look at the past to see if there have been companies that have lived the narrative that you are claiming for your company and what they share in common. The second is the test of experience, where you draw on investments based upon similar narratives that you have made in the past and remember or recognize road bumps and barriers that you ran into in practice. The third is the test of common sense, where you draw on first principles in economics and mathematics, to evaluate your narrative's weakest links."
>
> "Step 3: Convert key parts of the narrative into drivers of value: Ultimately, even the most gripping narratives have to show up in the numbers."
>
> "Step 4: Connect the drivers of value to a valuation: I use discounted cash flow models (DCF) to connect the drivers of value to value, because I am comfortable with the mechanics of these models."
>
> "Step 5: Keep the feedback loop open: ... The biggest enemy that we (whether numbers or narrative driven) face is hubris, where we get locked into our initial points of views and view changing our minds as a sign of weakness."
> — "Numbers and Narrative: Modeling, Story Telling and Investing", 24 Jun 2014, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/06/numbers-and-narrative-modeling-story.html

The class deck adds three rules for step 1 and lists what the narrative must cover: "Every valuation starts with a narrative, a story that you see unfolding for your company in the future. In developing this narrative, you will be making assessments of your company (its products, its management), the market or markets that you see it growing in, the competition it faces and will face and the macro environment in which it operates. Rule 1: Keep it simple. Rule 2: Keep it focused. Rule 3: Stay grounded in reality." — "Narrative and Numbers: Connecting Stories to Value" slides 255–257, https://people.stern.nyu.edu/adamodar/pdfiles/eqnotes/narrativeandnumbers.pdf

**The 3P test (step 2).** "Not everything that is possible is plausible, and not all plausible opportunities make the transition to the probable. As I see it, the divergence between the my narrative and Bill Gurley's are captured in where we draw the lines between the probable and the plausible and the value that we attach to the possible." — "Possible, Plausible and Probable: Big markets and Networking effects", 16 Jul 2014, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/07/possible-plausible-and-probable-big.html. The deck labels the three failure modes: "The Impossible: The Runaway Story", "The Implausible: The Big Market Delusion", "The Improbable: Willy Wonkitis" (slides 263–266). In 2022 he restated the test as the investor's duty: "the absence of numbers can make it difficult to take the story through the 3P test, where you evaluate whether it is possible, plausible and probable. It is your obligation as an investor to push for specificity, often in terms of the market that the business is targeting and the market share and profitability numbers that will determine its profitability." — "META Lesson 3: Tell me a story!", 15 Nov 2022, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2022/11/meta-lesson-3-tell-me-story.html

**Story to drivers (step 3).** The Uber slide is his canonical mapping (slide 267 of the same deck): total market ("The global taxi/limo business is $100 billion in 2013, growing at 6% a year") × market share ("Target market share is 10%") = revenues; minus operating expenses ("Target pre-tax operating margin is 40%"); minus reinvestment ("allowing it to maintain a high sales to capital ratio for the sector (5.00)"); "Adjust for time value & risk" ("high cost of capital (12%) up front. As it grows, it will become safer and its cost of capital will drop to 8%"); and "Adjusted for operating risk with a discount rate and for failure with a probability of failure" ("10% probability of failure"). In 2023 he compressed the whole map into one sentence: "the value of a company is a function of four broad inputs - revenue growth, as a stand-in for its growth potential, a target operating margin as a proxy for profitability, a reinvestment scalar (I use sales to invested capital) as a measure of the efficiency with which it delivers growth and a cost of capital & failure rate to incorporate risk." — Nvidia post, 23 Jun 2023, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2023/06/ais-winners-losers-and-wannabes-nvidia.html

**Drivers to value (step 4).** "With the story in place, and the inputs that come out of it, the valuation, in a sense, does itself" — Zomato, 22 Jul 2021, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2021/07/the-zomato-ipo-bet-on-big-markets-and.html

**Feedback loop (step 5).** He classifies what new information does to the story: "Narrative breaks/ends ... Narrative shifts ... Narrative changes (expansion/contraction)" and says earnings reports "are the vehicles that we should use to confirm, reject or modify narratives (and thus value)." — "Reacting to Earnings Reports: Narrative Adjustments and Value Effects", 6 Aug 2014, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/08/reacting-to-earnings-reports-narrative.html. His self-check on bias: "A test: If your valuations are unbiased, you should find yourself increasing estimated values as often as you are decreasing values." — "The Dark Side of Valuation: Valuing young, high growth companies", Lesson 8, https://people.stern.nyu.edu/adamodar/pdfiles/eqnotes/darksideyoung.pdf

Why the story has to come first, in his words: "every number you have in your valuation, from growth to margins to risk measures, should be backed up by a story about that number, and every story you tell about a company, including its great management, brand name or technological edge, has be reflected in a number in your valuation." And the payoff: "Stories allow for consistency-tests: When your valuation numbers come from a story, it becomes almost impossible to change one input to your valuation without thinking through how that change affects your story. An increase in revenue growth, in a company in a niche market with high margins, may require a recalibration of the story to make it a more mass-market story, albeit with lower margins." — META Lesson 3 (URL above).

## 2. Revenue growth

### Two engines: fundamentals for the mature, top-down for the young

"Look at fundamentals. With stable margins, operating income growth can be tied to how much a firm reinvests, and the returns it earns. With changing margins, you have to start with revenue growth, forecast margins and estimate reinvestment." — "Growth" deck, slide 163, https://people.stern.nyu.edu/adamodar/pdfiles/eqnotes/dcfgrowt.pdf. The mature-firm identity is "Reinvestment Rate X Return on Investment = Growth Rate in Earnings" (slide 179), with the warning: "In general, if return on capital and margins are changing and/or expected to change at a company, you are better off not using any of the sustainable growth equations to estimate growth." (slide 188)

The top-down recipe (slide 191):

> "When operating income is negative or margins are expected to change over time, we use a three-step process to estimate growth: Estimate growth rates in revenues over time — Determine the total market (given your business model) and estimate the market share that you think your company will earn. Decrease the growth rate as the firm becomes larger. Keep track of absolute revenues to make sure that the growth is feasible. Estimate expected operating margins each year — Set a target margin that the firm will move towards. Adjust the current margin towards the target margin. Estimate the capital that needs to be invested to generate revenue growth and expected margins — Estimate a sales to capital ratio that you will use to generate reinvestment needs each year."

The book gives two concrete ways to get the year-by-year path. First, work backwards from end-state share: "assume that you are analyzing an online toy retailer with $100 million in revenues currently. Assume also that the entire toy retail market had revenues of $70 billion last year. Assuming a 3% growth rate in this market over the next 10 years and a market share of 5% for your firm, you would arrive at expected revenues of $4.703 billion for the firm in ten years and a compounded revenue growth rate of 46.98%." Second, extrapolate then hand off to size-peers: "You could forecast growth rates of 120%, 100%, 80% and 60% for the next 4 years, leading to revenues of $1.267 billion in four years. You could then look at the average growth rate posted by retail firms with revenues between $1 and $1.5 billion last year and use that as the growth rate commencing in year 5." — *Investment Valuation* Ch. 11, "Market Size, Market Share and Revenue Growth", https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/valn2ed/ch11.pdf

Ch. 23 lists the sources he draws on for a young firm's growth rate: "Past growth rate in revenues at the firm itself: Since the firm increases in scale as it grows, it will become more and more difficult to maintain very high growth rates. Thus, a firm that grew 300% two years ago and 200% last year is likely to grow a lower rate this year. Growth rate in the overall market that the firm serves ... Barriers to Entry and Competitive Advantages possessed by the firm: For a firm to be able to sustain high growth rates, it has to have some sustainable competitive advantage ... If the competitive advantage looks sustainable, high growth is much more likely to last for a long period. If it is not, it will taper off much faster." — https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/pdfiles/valn2ed/ch23.pdf

### Five things he keeps in mind about the revenue path (Ch. 11)

> "The rate of growth in revenues will decrease as the firm's revenues increase. Thus, a ten-fold increase in revenues is entirely feasible for a firm with revenues of $2 million but unlikely for a firm with revenues of $2 billion.
> Compounded growth rates in revenues over time can seem low, but appearances are deceptive. A compounded growth rate in revenues of 40% over ten years will result in a 40-fold increase in revenues over the period.
> While growth rates in revenues may be the mechanism that you use to forecast future revenues, you do have to keep track of the dollar revenues to ensure that they are reasonable, given the size of the overall market that the firm operates in. If the projected revenues for a firm ten years out would give it a 90% or 100% share (or greater) of the overall market in a competitive market place, you clearly should reassess the revenue growth rate.
> Assumptions about revenue growth and operating margins have to be internally consistent. Firms can post higher growth rates in revenues by adopting more aggressive pricing strategies but the higher revenue growth will then be accompanied by lower margins.
> In coming up with an estimate of revenue growth, you have to make a number of subjective judgments about the nature of competition, the capacity of the firm that you are valuing to handle the revenue growth and the marketing capabilities of the firm."

The same rules appear as the young-growth-company checklist in the Part I packet: "Lower revenue growth rates, as revenues scale up. Keep track of dollar revenues, as you go through time, measuring against market size. If you set your growth period to be much longer than ten years, you are already building in the expectation that your firm is an exceptional firm." — slide 295, https://people.stern.nyu.edu/adamodar/pdfiles/eqnotes/valpacket1spr26.pdf

### Growth is a means, not an end; growth is not free

"the revenue growth rate, at least for me, is a means to an end, not an end in itself. A few of you did take issue with the fact that the growth rate that I used for the first five years dropped from 35%, in my November 2021 valuation, to 24%, in my most recent one. That may seem like a negative reassessment of the company, but growth rates can deceptive as companies get larger, and my revenues in 2032 in both valuations converge on almost exactly the same number ($420 billion in November 2021 valuation and $412 billion in this one)." — "Disagreements and First Principles: The Pushback on my Tesla Valuation", 2 Feb 2023, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2023/02/disagreements-and-first-principles.html

"Growth is not free and it has to be paid for with reinvestment" — "Myth 5.3: Growth is good, more growth is better!", 30 Nov 2016, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/11/myth-53-growth-is-good-more-growth-is.html. The deck: "Growth, though, is a double-edged sword. The good side of growth is that it pushes up revenues and operating income ... The bad side of growth is that you have to set aside money to reinvest to create that growth." (Growth deck, slide 162). The Dark Side deck's "Lesson 6: Don't forget to pay for growth…".

### The big market delusion

"overconfidence on the part of business founders and their capital providers can lead to unrealistic judgments of future profits, and overly high estimates for what they think their companies are worth, in what I will term the 'big market delusion'." Its "Common Elements": "Big Market stories ... they rarely express the need to go beyond that justification, by explaining why the specific company they were recommending was positioned to take advantage of that growth ... Blindness to competition ... All about growth ... Disconnect from fundamentals". — "The Market is Huge! Revisiting the Big Market Delusion", 29 Dec 2019, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2019/12/the-market-is-huge-revisiting-big.html

His aggregate check: back out the revenue each listed company must reach to justify its price and add them up. For online advertising in 2013 the sum was "$319.2 billion, higher than my estimate of the overall online advertising market in 2023", and the lesson: "Unless we consciously step back and look at the big picture, we will continue to create micro valuations that don't up aggregate to create viable macro environments." — "When the pieces add up to too much", 14 Oct 2013, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/10/when-pieces-dont-add-up-micro-dreams.html. He refuses a separate "big market premium": "that market size should already by incorporated into the intrinsic value, through growth and margin expectations. In my base case valuation of Zomato, I assume that revenues will increase almost ten-fold over the next 10 years, because the Indian market is expected to grow so strongly." — Zomato (URL above).

### Reading the latest quarter against history

He anchors on the trailing twelve months, not the last annual report: "When valuing Amazon in early 2000, the last annual report that was available was the 1998 annual report. For a young company, that is ancient data, since so much can change over the course of a short time period. To value Amazon the trailing 12-month numbers were used." — Dark Side deck, "Lesson 3: Use updated numbers". Ch. 23 footnote 3: "One simple approach is to scale all of the inputs to reflect the growth in revenues that has occurred between the last financial year and the trailing twelve months."

Recent quarters move the growth input directly: "I did lower my revenue growth rate to 1.50%, reflecting the bad news about revenues in the most recent 10Q." — "DCF Myth 3.2", 23 May 2016, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/05/dcf-myth-32-if-you-don-look-its-not.html. On margins he picks the quarter he thinks is representative rather than the annual average: "my operating margin is dropping from 17.93% to 16% over the next five years, but that is because the former is the operating margin during 2022, and the number careened wildly during the course of the year from more than 19% in the first quarter of the year, to just about 16% in the last quarter. In short, I am assuming that the price cuts and cost pressures of the fourth quarter are more representative of what Tesla will face in the future". But he does not let a cyclical dip drive the path: "Lowering revenue growth to 15% in 2023 and raising it to 33% in 2024 will deliver almost the same value for the company, as what I get with my smoothed-out values." — Tesla pushback post (URL above).

On fading to a company's own past average, his general warning about mean reversion is blunt: "The process of mean reversion is built on the presumption that the underlying distribution ... is stationary ... If there are structural changes that alter the underlying distribution, there is no quicker way to ruin that trusting in mean reversion." His example is a business-mix change: "retailing, where Amazon's entry and subsequent growth has laid waste to historic norms for this sector, bringing down operating margins and changing reinvestment patterns." — "Mean Reversion: Gravitational Super Force or Dangerous Delusion?", 31 Aug 2016, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/08/mean-reversion-gravitational-super.html. His own reset on Meta shows the same logic applied to a growth input: "revenue growth of 8% a year that I would reset to a lower number, with the recognition that online advertising is seeing growth level off, faster than I thought it would, and is more cyclical than I assumed it would be." — META Lesson 3.

### Companies with several businesses

He does not build line-item segment models by default, and says why: "It is true that I don't break revenues down, by business, but that reflects my view that breaking things into detail, without any real basis for forecasting detailed line items creates the illusion of precision, while actually making your valuation less so." — Tesla pushback post. He does split when the segments have different growth engines: for Nvidia he wrote "the businesses are diverging in terms of revenue growth potential" and built the revenue distribution "for each of the three segments of NVIDIA's revenues" (Nvidia 2023); his 2024 Mag-Seven table carries the footnote "NVidia and Tesla were valued as the sum of the valuations of their different businesses. The growth and margins reported are for the consolidated company." — "The Seven Samurai", 8 Feb 2024, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2024/02/the-seven-samurai-how-big-tech-rescued.html. For Amazon in 2018: "To value Amazon today, I think it makes sense to break it up into at least three parts, with the first being its retail/media business globally, the second its AWS business and finally, Amazon Prime." — https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2018/04/amazon-glimpses-of-shoeless-joe.html. For Alphabet's "bets" he did the opposite and folded them into the margin: "I have implicitly assumed that the company will continue to spend billions in its bets, by leaving the margin at existing levels; remember that the operating margin of 27.87% is after the company's spending on its bet businesses ... I do think that trying to build business models from scratch, to value Waymo, Verily and Nest is difficult to do right now". — https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2018/04/alphabet-soup-google-is-alpha-but-where.html

### How long high growth lasts

Covered in the companion note. His one-line version: "the company has ten years of growth, not five, but growth rates have to get smaller as revenues gets bigger" (Tesla pushback).

### Tests on end-year revenue

He always compares the year-10 revenue to real companies and to the market:

- "As a check, we also examined how much the revenues at each of these firms would be in ten years relative to more mature companies in the sector now. We compared revenues at Commerce One in ten years to those of EDS – a leading provider of business services- today. EDS had revenues of $18.73 billion in 1999, which would make Commerce One a leading player in this sector but not by an overwhelming margin." — Ch. 11.
- "I am going to assume ... that success will bring them revenues close to those delivered by a company like Audi ($64 billion). While it is conceivable that Tesla's revenues could approach those of the auto giants ($100 billion plus), I think the revenue growth required to get to those levels would be incompatible with the high operating margins that I will be assuming for Tesla." — "Valuation of the Week 1: A Tesla Test", 4 Sep 2013, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2013/09/valuation-of-week-1-tesla-test.html
- "in January 2023, there were only five companies in the world that reported annual revenues exceeding $400 billion ... while $400 billion in revenues is clearly plausible, it is a difficult target for a firm to hit, and while you can use a higher growth rate than I use, and arrive at end revenues of a $1 trillion or more, you are estimating revenues that no company in history has ever generated." — Tesla pushback post.
- Nvidia's headroom was argued the same way: "it has a modest market share of the overall semiconductor market, with revenues that are less than half of the revenues posted by Intel or TSMC." — Nvidia 2023.
- The breakeven version: "The most obvious case is when the breakeven revenues that you compute for your firm exceeds the total market for the products or services that it provides." — "Trillion Dollar Market Caps", 3 Dec 2025, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2025/12/trillion-dollar-market-caps-fairy-tale.html

## 3. Operating margin

### Picking the target

Start with the business the firm is actually in and its real competitors: "In estimating this margin, you should begin by looking at the business that the firm is in. While many new firms claim to be pioneers in their businesses and some believe that they have no competitors, it is more likely that they are the first to find a new way of delivering a product or service that was delivered through other channels before ... Using the average operating margin of competitors in the business may strike some as conservative ... This may be true but it is unlikely that the operating margins for internet retailers can be persistently higher than their brick-and-mortar counterparts. If they were, you would expect to see a migration of traditional retailers to online retailing and increased competition among online retailers on price and products driving the margin down." — Ch. 11, "Operating Margin Forecasts". Ch. 23: "Looking at the underlying business that this firm is in, consider its true competitors. For instance, while Commerce One is considered to be a B2B or e-commerce firm, it is ultimately a provider of business services and software."

The industry numbers come from his datasets (Ch. 11 ends the section with "margins.xls: This dataset on the web summarizes operating margins, by industry, for the United States."; current table at https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/margin.html with columns "Pre-tax, Pre-stock compensation Operating Margin", "Pre-tax Lease & R&D adj Margin", "EBITDAR&D/Sales", "R&D/Sales"). But he uses the distribution, not just the mean, and he chooses where in it the company sits:

- Amazon 2014: "the target margin at a number (7.36%) that reflects the overall retail business in 2014"; optimistic story "12.84% (the 75th percentile of retail/media firms)"; pessimistic "2.85%, the 25th percentile of large US retail/media firms". — "DCF Myth 2", 8 Aug 2015, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2015/08/dcf-myth-2-dcf-is-exercise-in-modeling.html
- Uber 2014: "I will assume that Uber's operating margin, in steady state, will be 40%, towards the top decile of technology companies." — Uber post (section 9).
- Nvidia 2023, own history over peers: "While my target R&D adjusted margin of 40% may look high, it is worth remembering that the company delivered 42.5% as margin in 2020 and 38.4% as margin in 2021."
- Airbnb 2020, bracketed by comparables: "I will assume that the operating margins will improve over the next decade to 25%, lower than Booking.com's 2019 operating margin of 35.48%, but higher than the margins for Expedia or the hotel business." — https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2020/12/the-sharing-economy-come-home-ipo-of.html
- Zomato 2021, business-model reasoning: "I will assume that pre-tax operating margins will trend towards 30%, largely because I believe that the market will be dominated by a few big players, but with the very real possibility that one rogue player that is unwilling to play the game can upend profitability."
- Tesla 2023, unit economics over pricing power: "The operating margins that you can deliver as a company depend on product quality and pricing power, but they are also underpinned by unit economics. The companies that deliver the highest margins incur very low costs in producing the next units that they sell, and that is why software companies, tobacco companies and Aramco have sky-high margins."

The margin must agree with the growth story: "There is a niche market story that can be used to justify higher operating margins at Tesla, but that story would be incompatible with it having revenues of $400 billion." — "Tesla in 2023", 26 Jan 2023, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2023/01/tesla-in-2023-return-to-reality-start.html

### When the target is reached

"Generally, the improvements in margins will be greatest in the earlier years (at least in percentage terms) and then taper off as the firm approaches maturity." His mechanical rule in the illustration: "The margin each year is computed as follows: (Margin this year + Target margin)/2" — Ch. 11, Illustration 11.13 and footnote 8. In Ch. 23: "we assumed that the marginal improvement will be greater in the first few years, but we do not forecast operating profits until 5 years from now." In practice he often sets the target well inside the horizon: "I increase NVIDIA's R&D adjusted margin to 35% next year, and target an operating margin of 40% in 2027, that it maintains in perpetuity after that." (Nvidia 2023); "target an operating margin of 12% by 2025" (Tesla DIY, Feb 2020, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2020/02/a-do-it-yourself-diy-valuation-of-tesla.html).

### Margins during heavy investment

His first move is to reclassify growth spending out of operating expenses, so the reported margin is not the starting margin: "Since the company can legitimately argue that some of these expenses (such as the R&D and customer acquisition costs) are more in the nature of capital expenditures than operating expenses, I will assume (generously) that the company generated an operating income of $10 million in the most recent 12 months." (Uber 2014). "Deconstruct the firm's current income statement to get a truer measure of its operating margin. Many young start-up firms that report negative earnings do so, not because their operating expenses from generating current revenues are large, but because accounting convention requires them to report capital expenses as operating expenses." (Ch. 23). On Meta: "Some of the bad news in Facebook's earnings report, especially relating to lower profitability, reflected accounting mis-categorization of R&D and expenses at Reality Labs (Facebook's Metaverse entree) as operating, rather than capital expenses." (META Lesson 3). On Amazon: "The operating margin for Amazon would have been over 20%, if it had not incurred these expenditures, but with those higher, the company would have had far less revenues, no AWS business and no Amazon Prime today." (Amazon 2018). The Dark Side deck's "Lesson 4: Many of the operating expenses may be capital expenses" adds the other half: "If we adopt this rationale, it will also mean that they are reinvesting far more than we think they are. It will, however, make not their cash flows less negative."

Second, he writes down the offsets explicitly when he assumes margin expansion. Operating leverage: "The biggest expenses at these companies are often on customer acquisition and marketing, and as growth scales down, these expenses should decrease, as a percent of revenues, delivering a profitability bonus." (Zomato). "its operating margins will continue to improve towards travel booking industry levels, as product development, marketing and G&A costs decrease, not in dollar terms, but as a percent of revenues." (Airbnb). "the improved margin reflect expected economies of scale." (Amazon retail 2018). But with a test: "Economies of scale will be a contributor to improving margins only if a company has significant operating expenses (SG&A, Marketing) that grow at a rate lower than revenues. To measure the potential for economies of scale, I looked at the difference between gross and operating margins, across industries, with the rationale that companies with a large difference have a greater potential for economies of scale." — Data Update 7 for 2025, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2025/02/data-update-7-for-2025-end-game-in.html. And "While growing can provide scaling benefits, that works only if the fixed costs don't grow with revenues and if they are not so onerous, that you still have losses after scaling up." — "The Scaling and Profitability Trade off", 2 Sep 2026, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/09/the-scaling-and-profitability-trade-off.html

Depreciation drag as a stand-alone topic: not found in his prose. The nearest statements are his use of the EBITDA-minus-operating-margin gap as a capital-intensity flag ("for businesses that have significant depreciation, that difference can be substantial. It is also a rough measure of capital intensity", Data Update 7 for 2025) and his treatment of hyperscaler capex, which the companion note covers (section 3c-ter there). In his own valuations the drag never appears in isolation because reinvestment is modelled through sales-to-capital rather than through the depreciation line.

Operating leverage and risk: he puts it in the beta, not only the margin. "One argument that can be made for why young firms should have much higher betas than larger, more mature firms in their business is that they have much higher operating leverage. The costs for young firms are for the most part fixed and do not vary with revenues ... adjust the bottom-up beta for differences in operating leverage ... Unlevered Beta = Business Beta ( 1 + (Fixed Costs/ Variable Costs))" — Ch. 23.

### R&D and leases

"Make sure that there are no capital expenses mixed in with the operating expenses. Capital expense: Any expense that is expected to generate benefits over multiple periods. There are a shole host of expenses (like R&D) that meet this description that accountants treat as operating expenses." And the recipe: "To capitalize R&D, Specify an amortizable life for R&D (2 - 10 years). Collect past R&D expenses for as long as the amortizable life. Sum up the unamortized R&D over the period." Leases: "Since they give rise to contractual commitments, operating lease expenses should be treated as financing expenses, with the following adjustments to earnings and capital: Debt Value of Operating Leases = Present value of Operating Lease Commitments at the pre-tax cost of debt ... Adjusted Operating Earnings = Operating Earnings + Operating Lease Expenses - Depreciation on Leased Asset". — "Cash flows" deck, slides 122–124, https://people.stern.nyu.edu/adamodar/pdfiles/eqnotes/dcfcf.pdf. Applied: "I capitalized R&D at NVIDIA, using a 5-year life, and recalculated my operating income (since the reported version is built on the accounting mis-reading of R&D as an operating expense) ... The R&D adjusted operating margin at semiconductor companies is higher by about 2-4%, in every time period" (Nvidia 2023); "In keeping with my view that R&D is really a capital expense, I capitalize R&D, which improves Tesla's profitability" (Tesla DIY 2020).

### Margin convergence

"as long as anticipated margins for e-commerce firms are higher than they are for traditional competitors, there will be increased competition coming from the latter, pushing margins towards convergence." — Ch. 23. Also the two reasons he refused a higher-than-industry margin for Commerce One: "The first is that the high growth rates in revenues that we have assumed will require aggressive pricing from Commerce One and, therefore, lower margins. The second is that as long as anticipated margins for e-commerce firms are higher than they are for traditional competitors, there will be increased competition".

## 4. Reinvestment

### Sales-to-capital: definition and sources

"To link revenue growth with reinvestment needs, you look at the revenues that every dollar of capital that you invest generates. This ratio, called the sales to capital ratio, allows us to estimate how much additional investment the firm has to make to generate the projected revenue growth. This investment can be in internal projects, acquisitions or working capital ... To estimate the sales to capital ratio, you look at both a firm's past and the business it operates in. To measure this ratio historically, you look at changes in revenue each year and divide it by the reinvestment made that year. You also look at the average ratio of sales to book capital invested in the business in which the firm operates." — Ch. 11. Ch. 23: "it can be estimated by looking at the firm's history, limited though it might be, and at industry averages, with the industry defined broadly to reflect the business the firm is in."

His worked table shows how he chooses among the three candidates (Ch. 11, Table 11.16): for Ashford.com, firm's ratio 1.18, marginal last year 1.60, industry 3.24, used 2.50, "approximately midway through their marginal sales to capital ratio from last year and the industry average"; for Commerce One, firm 3.13, marginal 2.70, industry 3.18, used 2.00, "well below the industry average and the firm's marginal sales to capital ratio. We feel that as competition increases, Commerce One will have to invest increasing amounts in technology and in acquisitions to grow."

The dataset (`capex.xls`, HTML at https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/capex.html) carries the column "Sales/ Invested Capital (LTM)" by industry; the page itself has no methodology prose. In the blog he quotes it as the reference distribution: "The median value for technology companies on this is about 2.50, the median value for all US companies is about 2.00 and a really high number would be about 10.00. I will assume that the sales to capital ratio for Uber will be 5.00, towards the high end of the spectrum." (Uber 2014). For Tesla 2013: "That matches the industry average of the sales to capital ratio of 1.41 for US companies. Since the sales to capital ratio for technology companies is higher (2.66), it is possible that I am over estimating Tesla's reinvestment in the early years." For Amazon 2000 the slide notes "Sales to capital ratio and expected margin are retail industry average numbers" (Dark Side deck, slide 7).

Reinvestment is defined broadly: "Reinvestment = (Cap Ex - Depreciation & Amortization) + Change in non-cash Working Capital + Acquisitions + (R&D expenses - Amortization of R&D)" — Data Update 6 for 2020, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2020/03/data-update-6-for-2020-profitability.html. "our experiences with technology companies (including Google, Twitter and Facebook) suggest that Uber will be forced to reinvest, especially as it scales up, and that this reinvestment will take the form of acquiring new technologies and companies." (Uber 2014)

### The implied return on capital check

This is the sanity check he insists on, quoted in full because the engine's analyst skipped it:

> "One of the dangers that you face when using a sales-to-capital ratio to generate reinvestment needs is that you might under-estimate or over-estimate your reinvestment needs. You can keep tabs on whether this is happening and correct it when it does by also estimating the after-tax return on capital on the firm each year through the analysis ... If the sales-to-capital ratio is set too high, the return-on-capital in the later years will be too high, while if it is set too low, it will be too low. Too low or high relative to what, you ask? There are two comparisons that are worth making. The first is to the average return-on-capital for mature firms in the business in which your firm operates ... The second is to the firm's own cost of capital. A projected return on capital of 40% for a firm with a cost of capital of 10% in a sector where returns on capital hover around 15% is an indicator that the firm is investing too little for the projected revenue growth and operating margins. Decreasing the sales to capital ratio until the return on capital converges on 15% would be prudent." — Ch. 11, "Link to Return on Capital"

He confirms it after the fact: "The returns-on-capital at both firms converge to sustainable levels, at least relative to industry averages, by the terminal year. This suggests that our estimates of sales to capital ratios are reasonable." (Ch. 11) and "If the return on capital had become much larger than the industry average in year 10, we would have lowered the sales to capital ratio used in the valuation." (Ch. 23, footnote 5). Applied to Tesla 2013: "the return on invested capital that I obtain for Tesla in steady state (in year 10), based on my estimates of operating income and invested capital, is 11.27%, putting it again at the top decile of automobile companies."

### When a company has invested ahead of growth

He reads the historical ratio as the product of past spending whose revenue has not yet arrived, and lets the forward ratio move toward the industry: "NVIDIA has invested heavily in the last decade, generating only 65 cents in revenues for every dollar of capital invested (including the investment in R&D), in 2022. That investment has clearly been productive, as the company has been able to find growth and generate excess returns. I believe that given the company's larger scale, with the payoff from past investments augmenting revenues, the company's sales to invested capital will approach the global industry median, which is $1.15 in revenues for every dollar of capital invested." (Nvidia 2023). With Tesla he did the mirror image, front-loading a high ratio while capacity was idle: "In the near term, though, I assume less reinvestment, assuming $3 in revenues for every new dollar of capital invested, since Tesla contends in its January 2020 earnings call to have capacity online to produce 640,000 cars, enough to cover growth for the next year or two." — "An Ode to Luck", 30 Jan 2020, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2020/01/an-ode-to-luck-revisiting-my-tesla.html; and "To the extent that the company has the excess capacity to cover growth for the next few years, I will allow for a a higher sales to capital ratio in the early years, but move it towards a more sustainable number thereafter." — Tesla Nov 2021, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2021/11/teslas-trillion-dollar-moment-valuation.html

The deck's general point: "While sustainable growth equations are stated in terms of returns on capital (equity) or sales to capital the numbers that drive growth are returns on new investments, i.e., marginal returns on capital (equity) or marginal sales to capital ratios ... As companies scale up, the marginal values for these variables can diverge from the aggregate values. For companies where there are investing economies to scale, the marginal values can be significantly higher than the aggregate values. For companies that are facing changing competitor or are entering new businesses, the marginal values can be lower than the aggregate values." — Growth deck, slide 199. (The explicit reinvestment lag is in the companion note.)

How much it matters: "The first is the reinvestment each year, that I estimate using a sales to capital ratio that varies across time. It affects my cash flows, but its effect on value is dwarfed by changes in assumptions about market size and share." (Zomato). For a high-growth company it is not negligible, though: on Amazon 2014 he told a commenter the ratio was 3.68 and that lowering it "can be devastating" (WebFetch summary of https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/10/if-you-build-it-revenues-they-profits.html; verbatim wording of that comment not captured).

## 5. Cost of capital

**Bottom-up beta.** "Step 1: Find the business or businesses that your firm operates in. Step 2: Find publicly traded firms in each of these businesses and obtain their regression betas. Compute the simple average across these regression betas ... Unlever this average beta using the average debt to equity ratio across the publicly traded firms in the sample ... Step 3: Estimate how much value your firm derives from each of the different businesses it is in. Step 4: Compute a weighted average of the unlevered betas ... Step 5: Compute a levered beta (equity beta) for your firm, using the market debt to equity ratio for your firm." Why: "Less Noisy: The standard error in a bottom-up beta will be significantly lower than the standard error in a single regression beta ... Updated: The bottom-up beta can be adjusted to reflect changes in the firm's business mix and financial leverage. Regression betas reflect the past." — "Discount Rates" deck, slides 92–93, https://people.stern.nyu.edu/adamodar/pdfiles/eqnotes/dcfrates.pdf. "I use industry average betas, adjusted for leverage, rather than the company regression beta, because because the averages (I title them bottom up betas) are significantly better at explaining differences in returns across stocks." — Data Update 6 for 2025, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2025/02/data-update-6-for-2025-from-macro-to.html

**ERP.** The deck's synthesis slide (97) calls for "Preferably, a bottom-up beta, based upon other firms in the business, and firm's own financial leverage" and an "Implied Premium: Based on how equity market is priced today and a simple valuation model". "since my riskfree rate is always the current rate, and my equity risk premiums are implied, i.e., they are backed out from how stocks are priced, my estimates of costs of capital represent market prices for risk, not theoretical models. Thus, if looking at the table, you decide that a number (median for your region, 90th percentile in US) look too low or too high, your issues are with the market, not with me (or my assumptions)." — Data Update 6 for 2025.

**The distribution idea.** "While I cannot provide you with the company-level costs of capital, I can provide the cross sectional distribution of my estimates. As you look at companies, I hope that you can use this for perspective, i.e., in making judgments on what comprises a high, low and median cost of capital ... Thus, if you use a cost of capital of 10% in the United States, you would effectively be assuming that your company is in the 98th percentile of US companies". — "Putting the D in the DCF", 19 Jan 2015, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2015/01/putting-d-in-dcf-cost-of-capital.html. "The median US $ cost of capital for a US company is 7.22%, 50% of all US companies have costs of capital between 5.69% and 8.14%, and 80% have costs of capital between 4.59% and 8.87%." — Data Update 6, 26 Jan 2017, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2017/01/january-2017-data-update-6-cost-of.html. "half of all US companies have costs of capital between 7.3% and 10.9%" (Tesla pushback, 2023). The exact "6–10%" phrase was not found; the closest is "the cost of capital, even if you go with the global distribution, varies within a tight range (6% to 12%, if you use the 10th and 90th percentile)" (2015).

He uses the table as an input generator (young money-losers at the 90th percentile, stable European staples at the 25th; Data Update 5 for 2020, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2020/02/data-update-5-relative-risk-and-hurdle.html): "if I am valuing a small, AI firm that has just gone public and has global operations, in US dollars, I will start the valuation with a cost of capital of 11.66% and move that cost of capital over time towards 8.65%, as its gets larger and more established." — Data Update 5 for 2026, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/02/data-update-5-for-2026-risk-and-hurdle.html. And as a plausibility gate: "In the context of checking to see whether a valuation passes the 3P test (Is it possible? Is it plausible? Is it probable?), I do check the cost of capital used in the valuation." (2025)

**The rule against fine-tuning.** "Don't sweat the small stuff: In my view, analysts spend too much time finessing and tweaking the cost of capital and not enough on the cash flows ... profit margins and returns on capital have a much wider distribution across companies and getting those numbers right has a much bigger pay off." (2015). "if I am valuing an average risk company in US dollars, I will start off using an 8% cost of capital (the global median is 8.03%) and complete my valuation with that number, and if I still have time, I will come back and tweak the cost of capital." (2017). "Estimating risk free rates, betas and equity risk premiums to the second, third or even fourth decimal points offers the illusion of control" — "Myth 4.2", 4 Nov 2016, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/11/myth-42-it-all-about-d-in-dcf.html. "the culprit almost always has been an error on forecasting growth, profitability or reinvestment (all of which lead into the cash flows), not the discount rate." (2025). "costs of capital of 15% or 6% are just off the table." (Tesla pushback).

**Two things the discount rate must not carry.** Failure risk: "Don't try to incorporate failure risk into the discount rate. Let the cost of capital change over time, as the company changes. If you are desperate, use the cross section of costs of capital to get your estimation going (use the 90th or 95th percentile across all companies)." — Part I packet, slide 292–293. Management quality: "Some analysts use them to bring in the quality of management ... Venture capitalists pump up discount rates to compensate themselves for failure risk ... they are dangerous for many reasons: you can double count both the good and bad, you may be adjusting for risks you should not be and biasing your valuations." — "Myth 4.4", https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/11/myth-44-ddiscount-rate-is-receptacle.html

**Time-varying.** "Whatever approach we use to estimate costs of equity and debt, they should not be left unchanged over the estimation period. As the firm matures and moves towards its sustainable margin and stable growth, the risk parameters should also approach those of an average firm – the betas should move towards one and the cost of debt should adjust towards the industry average cost of debt." — Ch. 23.

## 6. Terminal value discipline (already covered elsewhere; one paragraph)

Growth in perpetuity is capped by the economy and he uses the risk-free rate as its proxy: "The second is the question of what the nominal growth in the global economy, in US dollar terms, will be, and my best answer to that question is the nominal risk free rate" (Tesla pushback); the response to "too pessimistic" is "not to increase the growth rate from 3.47% to a higher value after year 10, but to either use higher growth in the next ten years". The cost of capital in stable growth is the mature firm's (Ch. 23 quote above). Excess returns: "I adopt the practice of assuming that excess returns will move towards zero in stable growth and giving myself discretion on how much, with zero excess return being my choice for firms with few or no sustainable competitive advantages, a positive excess return for firms with strong and sustainable competitive advantages" (Myth 5.3). The two practices he bans: "Grow the nth year's cash flow" and "Stable Growth firms don't need to reinvest" (Myth 5.3). Detail is in the companion note.

## 7. Bear/base/bull and probabilities

**He builds alternative stories, not alternative numbers.** The Uber table in June 2014 had four rows, each a narrative with its own market, share, take rate, cost of capital and failure probability ("Car service company, facing significant competitive and regulatory hurdles ... $100 billion, 10%, 10%, 12%, 10%, $3.2 billion"; "Logistics company ... $600 billion, 5%, 20%, 12% ->8%, 0%, $17.5 billion"), and the lesson: "big differences in valuation almost always result from differing narratives about companies, not disagreements about the 'small stuff'." (Numbers and Narrative, 2014). For Amazon the bull and bear are different stories with the industry percentiles attached: "an alternative narrative, where Amazon's market power is unchecked ... operating margin of 12.84% (the 75th percentile of retail/media firms)"; "the pessimistic narrative ... paper-thin operating margins (2.85%, the 25th percentile of large US retail/media firms)". (Myth 2)

**Does he weight them?** In his company valuations he does not assign probabilities to discrete bear/base/bull cases; he reports the base case as expected value and puts the uncertainty into a Monte Carlo. "In a base case valuation, you do a valuation with your best estimates for the inputs into value from revenue growth to margins to risk measures. Much as you will be tempted to use conservative estimates, you should avoid the temptation and make your judgments on expected values." Then: "Step 2: Identify your driver variables ... focus on the variable that investors in the company seem to be most in disagreement about ... Step 4: Distributional Assumptions ... Step 5: Build in constraints and correlations". (Myth 3.2). Which inputs get distributions: "my suggestion is that you focus on the variables that not only matter the most, but where you feel most uncertain." What the simulation is for: "This is not about getting better estimates of value ... the median values across 100,000 simulations are close to my base case valuations ... There can be no one margin of safety for all companies". — "Amazon and Apple at a Trillion: A Follow-up", 21 Sep 2018, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2018/09/amazon-and-apple-at-trillion-follow-up.html. In Nvidia 2023 the distributions were: revenues by segment; target margin "triangular ... upper end of the range at 50% ... lower end at 30%"; sales to capital between the historic 0.65 and "1.94, the 75th percentile of the semiconductor business"; cost of capital centred on 12.21% "towards the market-average of 8.85% ... towards 15%, the 90th percentile of global companies". The verdict is a percentile, not a weighted average: "the current stock price is pushing towards the 95th percentile of my value distribution."

On the practice of unweighted best/base/worst he is scathing: "In its most sloppy form, and one that has been around for decades, scenario analysis has taken the form of best case - base case - worst case scenarios, an almost useless exercise, since there are almost no risky investments that are going to pass muster under the worst case scenario, no matter how good they are, or are going to fail under the best case scenario, no matter how bad they are. A scenario analysis, done right, should look at scenarios that cover all possible outcomes on an investment or decision, and for completion, need probabilities attached to the[m]". His workaround when probabilities are not estimable is to sort scenarios into possible / plausible / probable. — "AI Scenarios", 4 Mar 2026, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/03/ai-scenarios-from-economic-doomsday-to.html. Tool by narrative type: "Narrative Break/End ... Estimate a probability that it will occur & consequences. Narrative Shift ... Monte Carlo simulations or scenario analysis. Narrative Change ... Real Options" (Narrative deck, slide 272).

**Failure is a separate probability, outside the scenarios.** "Value of firm = Probability of surviving as a going concern * Discounted Cash flow value of firm + (1- Probability of surviving as a going concern) * Distress or Liquidation sale value" (Ch. 23). Uber 2014: "I will assume that there is only a 10% chance of failure in Uber and that the company's liquidation value will be zero." Zomato: "it is not a start-up, facing imminent failure ... Overall, I will attach a likelihood of failure of 10%".

**Management case and guidance.** He treats the company's story as an input to be tested, never as a scenario to be adopted: "you and I have to develop our own narratives, sometimes in sync with and sometimes at odds with the management story line" (2014); "company owners/managers cannot be given free rein over narratives" (Uber 2014). The class deck names the trap: "The Aggressive Analyst: Trust the firm to tell the truth and value the firm based upon the firm's statements about their value. The Conservative Analyst: Don't value what you cannot see." — Part I packet, slide 237. On guidance itself: "almost all of the information that I find useful in an earnings reports is in the financial statements and footnotes, not in the lengthy discussions of risk exposure or in the management guidance" — "The Gaming of Guidance", 29 Jul 2026, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2026/07/information-timing-and-release-gaming.html. Prospectus TAMs get the same treatment: Uber's "$2 trillion ... Big market stories generally yield higher valuation and pricing than small market stories!" (Uber IPO 2019, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2019/04/ubers-coming-out-party-personal.html); Airbnb's "$3.4 trillion" TAM was replaced with "Airbnb's targetable market falls somewhere in the middle, clearly higher than just the hotel business of $600 billion, but below Airbnb's upper end estimate of $2 trillion" (Airbnb 2020). Sell-side estimates are useful only as a reading of consensus: "There is value to knowing what analysts are forecasting as earnings growth for a firm. There is, however, danger when they agree too much (lemmingitis) and when they agree to little" (Growth deck, slide 176). An explicit "management case" as a named scenario in his own valuations: not found.

## 8. His own error lists

**Ten myths about the DCF model** (each later got its own post): "Myth 1: If you have a D(discount rate) and a CF (cash flow), you have a DCF ... Myth 2: A DCF is an exercise in modeling & number crunching. There is no room for creativity or qualitative factors. Myth 3: You cannot do a DCF when there is too much uncertainty ... Myth 4: The most critical input in a DCF is the discount rate and if you don't believe in modern portfolio theory (or beta), you cannot use a DCF. Myth 5: If most of your value in a DCF comes from the terminal value, there is something wrong with your DCF ... Myth 6: A DCF requires too many assumptions and can be manipulated to yield any value you want. Myth 7: A DCF cannot value brand name or other intangibles. Myth 8: A DCF yields a conservative estimate of value. It is better to under estimate value than over estimate it. Myth 9: If your DCF value changes significantly over time, there is either something wrong with your valuation ... or it is pointless ... Myth 10: A DCF is an academic exercise". — "Discounted Cashflow Valuations (DCF): Academic Exercise, Sales Pitch or Investor Tool?", 1 Feb 2015, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2015/02/discounted-cashflow-valuations-dcf.html. Sub-myths: 3.1 "The Margin of Safety: Tool for action or excuse for inaction?", 3.2 "If you don't look, its not there!"; 4.1 "If you don't like betas or modern portfolio theory, you cannot do a DCF", 4.2 "It's all about D in the DCF", 4.3 "The D cannot change over time in a DCF", 4.4 "The D(discount) rate is a receptacle for your hopes and fears", 4.5 "DCFs break down with near-zero risk free rates"; 5.1 "If you don't believe in forever, you cannot do a DCF", 5.2 "As g -> r, to infinity and beyond", 5.3 "Growth is good, more growth is better!", 5.4 "Negative growth rates forever? Impossible!", 5.5 "The terminal value ate my DCF!" (all Nov 2016, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2016/11/).

**Sins of DCF's proponents** (same 2015 post): "Over complicating DCF ... layers of detail that we not only don't need, but also make no difference to the ultimate value ... Over selling DCF ... Over sanitizing DCF ... cover up the uncertainties that are embedded into every valuation".

**Three dangers of numbers without narrative** (2014): "First, valuations become plug-and-point exercises ... Second, if a valuation is built around line items and individual inputs, there is a strong possibility that you may be creating a business that can exist only in spreadsheet nirvana, where revenues double every year, margins expand without challenge and growth comes without significant reinvestment. Finally, discussions and debates about inputs become shallow exercises in quibbling about the 'right' values to use, with no logical tie breaker."

**Four dangers of a just-the-numbers valuation** (META Lesson 3, 2022): "Play with numbers ... It is not uncommon to see analysts, when they estimate a value that they think is 'too low', to increase the revenue growth rate for a company, holding all else constant ... False precision ... From estimating the cost of capital to the fourth decimal point to forecasting all three accounting statements ... for the next 20 years ... Drown in data ... Denial of biases".

**The dark side's three temptations** (Dark Side deck, slide 4): "'Paradigm shifts' happen… New metrics are invented … The story dominates and the numbers lag…"

**Nine lessons from Amazon 2000** (Dark Side deck): "Lesson 1: Don't trust regression betas…. Lesson 2: The cost of capital will change over time… Lesson 3: Use updated numbers and the free cash flows will often be negative (even if the company is making money) ... Lesson 4: Many of the operating expenses may be capital expenses…. Lesson 5: Work backwards ... Lesson 6: Don't forget to pay for growth… Lesson 7: There are always scenarios where the market price can be justified… Lesson 8: You will be wrong 100% of the time… and it really is not (always) your fault… Lesson 9: Here is your consolation price… the market makes even bigger adjustments…"

**Analyst pathologies** (Growth deck, slide 175): "Tunnel Vision ... Lemmingitis ... Stockholm Syndrome: Refers to analysts who start identifying with the managers of the firms that they are supposed to follow. Factophobia (generally is coupled with delusions of being a famous story teller): Tendency to base a recommendation on a 'story' coupled with a refusal to face the facts. Dr. Jekyll/Mr.Hyde".

**Two dangerous terminal-value practices** (Myth 5.3): "1. Grow the nth year's cash flow ... 2. Stable Growth firms don't need to reinvest".

**Where the big mistakes actually are**: "When you make mistakes in valuation, it is almost always true that the big mistakes are in your cash flow and growth estimates, rather than in your cost of capital." — Data Update 6, 26 Jan 2018, https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2018/01/january-2018-data-update-6-cost-of.html

## 9. Two worked walkthroughs in his own words

### Uber, June 2014 — "A Disruptive Cab Ride to Riches: The Uber Payoff"

https://aswathdamodaran.blogspot.com/2014/06/a-disruptive-cab-ride-to-riches-uber.html

The story: "Uber is not in the taxi business, at least in the conventional sense, since it owns no cabs and has no cab drivers as employees. Instead, it plays the role of matchmaker, matching a driver/car with a customer looking for a ride and taking a slice of the fare for providing the service." Restated in the deck (slide 258): "An urban car service business ... Which would expand the business moderately (about 40% over ten years) by bringing in new users. With local networking benefits ... Maintain its revenue sharing (20%) system due to strong competitive advantages (from being a first mover). And its existing low-capital business model".

Base year: "the December leaks suggested that the company generated gross receipts ... of $1.1 billion, which would translate into revenues of $220 million (based on the 20% slice that Uber claims for itself) ... I will be using $1.5 billion for the gross receipts and $300 million as revenues for Uber as base year numbers." Operating income "(generously) ... $10 million in the most recent 12 months. (The effect on value of changing this number is relatively small)."

| Total market | $100bn in 2013, growing 6%/yr to $183bn in 2024 | "the most lucrative cab market in the world is in Japan ... $20-$25 billion annually just in Tokyo, followed by the UK (with revenues of $14 billion ...) and the US (with $11 billion ...). Making a judgment that taxi revenues currently in the rest of the world will add another $50 billion to this total, I arrive at a total market for taxi and limo services of $100 billion ... the taxi business globally will continue to grow at well above the 2-3% rate that we have observed in the US, Japan and UK, and that services like Uber will contribute to the faster growth. I will estimate an expected growth rate of 6% a year for the next decade" |

## 10. What the engine's analyst gets wrong that he would not

Prescriptive rules, each tied to a quote above.

1. **Start from the trailing twelve months, and do not step down year-1 growth without a story reason.** He uses "the trailing 12-month numbers" and scales inputs "to reflect the growth in revenues that has occurred between the last financial year and the trailing twelve months" (Dark Side lesson 3; Ch. 23 fn. 3). A lower year-1 rate is legitimate only when the latest quarters justify it ("I did lower my revenue growth rate to 1.50%, reflecting the bad news about revenues in the most recent 10Q") or when a temporary dip is being smoothed, which he shows barely moves value ("Lowering revenue growth to 15% in 2023 and raising it to 33% in 2024 will deliver almost the same value"). Absent either, the year-1 number should sit at or near the current run-rate and the fade should come from scale, not from an unexplained haircut. Addresses failure (a).

2. **Never fade toward a company's own five-year average when its business mix has changed; fade toward the end-state the story implies.** "If there are structural changes that alter the underlying distribution, there is no quicker way to ruin that trusting in mean reversion" (2016). His own practice is to set the year-10 revenue from market size × share, or from the largest firms in the sector ("only five companies in the world that reported annual revenues exceeding $400 billion"), and to back out the growth path: "the revenue growth rate ... is a means to an end, not an end in itself." Addresses failure (b).

3. **Choose sales-to-capital from three candidates (firm history, last-year marginal ratio, industry average) and explain the position; during an investment surge, treat the same-year ratio as a depressed marginal value and move toward the industry.** Ch. 11 Table 11.16 shows the choice and the reasons; Nvidia shows the surge case: "generating only 65 cents in revenues for every dollar of capital invested ... with the payoff from past investments augmenting revenues, the company's sales to invested capital will approach the global industry median". Then run the implied-ROIC check and adjust: "If the sales-to-capital ratio is set too high, the return-on-capital in the later years will be too high ... Decreasing the sales to capital ratio until the return on capital converges on 15% would be prudent." Addresses failure (c).

4. **When you model a margin drag from investment, model the offsets in the same breath, and reclassify growth spending before you start.** He first strips capitalised items out of operating expense ("Deconstruct the firm's current income statement to get a truer measure of its operating margin"; R&D adjustment "higher by about 2-4%"), then states the offsets explicitly and tests them ("Economies of scale will be a contributor to improving margins only if a company has significant operating expenses (SG&A, Marketing) that grow at a rate lower than revenues"; "as product development, marketing and G&A costs decrease, not in dollar terms, but as a percent of revenues"). A margin path that shows only the depreciation line rising and none of these is a one-sided story. Addresses failure (d).

5. **Pick the terminal return on capital from the story's competitive-advantage claim, and check that the last explicit year already converges to it.** "excess returns will move towards zero in stable growth and giving myself discretion on how much, with zero excess return being my choice for firms with few or no sustainable competitive advantages, a positive excess return for firms with strong and sustainable competitive advantages" (Myth 5.3). Ch. 11's test is that "The returns-on-capital at both firms converge to sustainable levels, at least relative to industry averages, by the terminal year"; if year 5 ROIC is far above the terminal ROIC, either the terminal number is too low for the story or the sales-to-capital is too high. A cash-flow cliff after year 5 is the symptom Ch. 23 fixes by moving ROIC to the industry average in year 11 rather than to the cost of capital in one step. Addresses failure (e); detail in the companion note.

6. **Write the story first and make every number quote it; place every input inside a named distribution.** "every number you have in your valuation, from growth to margins to risk measures, should be backed up by a story about that number" (Meta 2022). He never says "40%"; he says "40%, towards the top decile of technology companies", "12% (the top decile of US companies)", "1.15 ... the global industry median". The engine's `datasets.py` already carries his industry tables; the analyst should cite the percentile it is choosing and why the company sits there.

7. **Run the end-year revenue test and the growth-margin consistency test, and state the results.** "we also examined how much the revenues at each of these firms would be in ten years relative to more mature companies in the sector now" (Ch. 11); "Firms can post higher growth rates in revenues by adopting more aggressive pricing strategies but the higher revenue growth will then be accompanied by lower margins" (Ch. 11).

8. **Do not fine-tune the cost of capital, and do not load it with failure or management quality.** Start from the distribution, keep it inside the plausible band ("costs of capital of 15% or 6% are just off the table"), let it drift to the median as the firm matures, and put failure in a separate probability ("Don't try to incorporate failure risk into the discount rate").

9. **Bear/base/bull must be different stories, not one story with the growth dial moved; the base case is the expected value, not a conservative case; management's numbers are a narrative to test, not a fourth scenario to average in.** "big differences in valuation almost always result from differing narratives about companies, not disagreements about the 'small stuff'"; "Much as you will be tempted to use conservative estimates, you should avoid the temptation and make your judgments on expected values"; "sometimes in sync with and sometimes at odds with the management story line".

10. **Audit your own bias over time.** "If your valuations are unbiased, you should find yourself increasing estimated values as often as you are decreasing values."
