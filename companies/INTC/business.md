# Intel Corporation — The Business
_As of Q2 2026 (quarter ended June 27, 2026). Written 2026-09-07._

## 1. What they do

Intel designs central processing units (CPUs), the chips that run the programs; x86 CPUs, Intel's and AMD's, run most of the world's PCs and a large share of its servers, and unlike nearly every rival Intel also owns its factories [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Its customers (computer makers, contract manufacturers, cloud companies) order on purchase orders they can "cancel, change or delay product purchase commitments with little or no notice to us and without penalty" [10-K FY2025, Item 1].

Since 2024 Intel has been two businesses in one company. Intel Products designs and sells chips through two groups: the PC group, renamed in Q2 2026 (quarter ended June 27, 2026) from Client Computing Group (CCG) to Client Computing and Physical AI Group (CCPG) without restating numbers, and Data Center and AI (DCAI) [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 2]. Intel Foundry runs the factories, develops each new manufacturing process (a "node", such as Intel 18A) and the packaging that joins several chips into one, and is meant to serve outsiders too; today "substantially all" of its work is for Intel Products [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. Intel calls itself the only US company doing both leading-edge chip research and high-volume manufacturing, "a strategically important company from both a national economic and national security perspective" [10-K FY2025, Item 1].

How they got here. The x86 design behind Intel's CPUs has been "a foundational computing platform for over four decades", and its software base is why it is hard to displace [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. From 2021 to 2024, under CEO Pat Gelsinger, Intel spent heavily to catch up with TSMC (the Taiwanese contract chipmaker), its "leading manufacturing competitor", while revenue fell from $79.0 billion in FY2021 to $52.9 billion in FY2025 as PC sales dropped and data-center budgets moved to GPUs (graphics processing units, the chips NVIDIA sells for AI), a market "where we have been unsuccessful to date in becoming a meaningful participant" [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 1A; Item 8; 10-K FY2023, Item 8]. Lip-Bu Tan became CEO on March 18, 2025, cut jobs and capital spending, sold new shares to the US government, SoftBank and NVIDIA, and sold 51% of Altera [10-K FY2025, Item 7; DEF 14A 2026, CD&A]. By Q2 2026 a shortage of server CPUs for AI data centers had turned things: revenue grew 25% year on year, "our strongest revenue growth in more than fifteen years" [Q2 2026 release].

## 2. How the money comes in

**CCPG, the PC chips.** Customers are distributors and PC makers [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Revenue was $32.2 billion in FY2025 with $9.3 billion of segment operating income, the profit engine [10-K FY2025, Note 3]. In Q2 2026 Intel sold 8% fewer PC chips than a year earlier at prices 27% higher, mostly from a richer mix of premium products, and "market demand exceeded our available product supply" [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2].

**DCAI, the server chips.** Customers are the largest cloud companies ("hyperscalers") and server makers [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Revenue was $16.9 billion in FY2025 and $6.3 billion in Q2 2026 alone, up 59% year on year as server prices rose 48% and volume 9% [10-K FY2025, Note 3; 10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2].

**Intel Foundry, and why its revenue is mostly Intel paying itself.** Foundry reported $17.8 billion of revenue in FY2025 and $5.8 billion in Q2 2026, but only $307 million and $293 million came from outside customers [10-K FY2025, Note 3; 10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. The rest is internal: since Q1 2024 the factories "sell" wafers to Intel Products "at prices that are intended to approximate market pricing", because the two sides "are meant to reflect separate fabless semiconductor and foundry companies" (fabless: a chip designer with no factories) [10-K FY2025, Note 3]. Adding up the segments removes that internal sale on the "intersegment eliminations" line, so it never reaches consolidated (company-wide) revenue [10-K FY2025, Note 3]. The point: show what a stand-alone chip designer would earn and a stand-alone factory would lose. Outside revenue rose in 2026 mainly because Altera, half-sold in September 2025, now buys as a customer, $181 million in Q2 2026 [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2].

**All other, and Corporate unallocated.** "All other" holds Mobileye (driver-assistance chips), the IMS tool business (machines that make the masks used to print chips) and, through September 11, 2025, Altera (programmable chips); "Corporate unallocated" is not a business but unassigned costs, mainly restructuring (layoff and shutdown costs), stock pay and acquisition costs [10-K FY2025, Note 3].

**Three incompatible sets of segment numbers.** Intel redrew its segments in 2023, 2024 and 2025, so the table keeps each version separate [10-K FY2023, Note 3; 10-K FY2024, Note 3; 10-K FY2025, Note 3]. The FY2024 and FY2025 10-Ks even give different Foundry outside revenue for 2023 ($953 million versus $547 million) and do not reconcile them [10-K FY2024, Note 3; 10-K FY2025, Note 3].

## 3. The economics

Intel is two cost structures glued together, both mostly fixed. (Gross margin is the share of revenue left after the direct cost of making the chips; operating margin is what is left after R&D and overhead too.) Designing a chip is a fixed cost: R&D was $13.8 billion in FY2025, 26% of revenue, and does not rise with each extra chip sold [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. Owning factories is mostly fixed too: buildings and machines are depreciated (cost booked as a yearly expense) whether or not wafers run through them, and most of Intel's depreciation sits in Foundry [10-K FY2025, Note 3]. Intel's own words: "our gross margin and operating income can be disproportionately affected due to our high fixed cost structure, which is difficult to reduce quickly in response to lower demand" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. So cost per chip falls when factories are full and rises when they are not, a large part of why gross margin slid from 55.4% in FY2021 to 32.7% in FY2024 as revenue fell by a third, then recovered to 40.4% in Q2 2026 [10-K FY2023, Item 7; 10-K FY2025, Item 7; Q2 2026 release].

**Why Foundry loses about $2 billion a quarter.** The operating loss was $10.3 billion in FY2025 and $2.1 billion in Q2 2026 [10-K FY2025, Note 3; 10-Q Q2 2026, Note 2]. Two causes. A new node costs more before it costs less, through "higher depreciation costs and lower yields" (yield: the share of chips on a wafer that work); in Q2 2026 "an increased mix of higher-cost wafers manufactured on our Intel 18A process node" ate the gains on older nodes [10-K FY2025, Item 1A; 10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. The factories are not full: $34.5 billion of plant was still under construction at year-end 2025, and unless revenue grows to absorb its depreciation as it enters service, "our gross margin and operating income will be unfavorably affected" [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. For the loss to shrink, Intel Products must buy more wafers, outside customers must fill spare capacity, and 18A and then 14A must reach mature yields; the Q2 improvement came from "higher yields, improved cycle times and increased factory scale" [Q2 2026 call, p.4].

**Capital intensity.** Gross capital spending ran at 39% to 48% of revenue in FY2022–FY2024 while Intel built "shell ahead" capacity, then fell to 33% in FY2025 under "a more disciplined capital deployment strategy" [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 8; 10-K FY2023, Item 8]. Two things soften the bill: government grants and tax credits cut recorded plant cost by $6.7 billion in 2025, and financial partners paid for 49% of two factory projects (Apollo: Fab 34 in Ireland; Brookfield: two new fabs in Arizona) in return for 49% of their profits, the "non-controlling interests" in this report [10-K FY2025, Note 6; Note 4; 10-Q Q2 2026, Note 3]. Management now expects 2026 capex of "more than $20 billion" and 2027 "significantly above the 2026 levels" [Q2 2026 call, p.5].

## 4. How profitable, really

Operating cash flow fell from $29.5 billion in FY2021 to $8.3 billion in FY2024 while gross capital spending stayed above $23 billion, so Intel's own "adjusted free cash flow" was negative four years running [10-K FY2023, Item 8; 10-K FY2025, Item 7]. That measure counts capex net of what governments and factory partners paid in [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. This flattered FY2024, when Apollo's $11.0 billion for the Ireland stake and Brookfield's payments added $12.7 billion, and punished the first half of 2026, when buying Apollo out for $14.2 billion turned the same line into a $10.3 billion outflow (computed) [10-K FY2025, Note 4; 10-Q Q2 2026, Note 3; Q2 2026 release]. Without the buyout, six-month operating cash flow of $8.1 billion roughly covered gross capex of $7.6 billion (computed) [Q2 2026 release].

**The $(11.0) billion GAAP loss in Q2 2026 is an accounting effect, not cash.** Intel earned $1.8 billion of operating income yet reported a net loss of $11.0 billion, or $(2.16) a share [Q2 2026 release]. The cause is the 159 million "Escrowed Shares" set aside for the US government, released at $20.00 a share as money from Secure Enclave, a program under the CHIPS Act (the 2022 US law that pays grants for chip factories), arrives [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. The promise to hand over those shares is booked as a liability at their current market value, re-measured every quarter; because Intel's share price rose, it climbed from $2.7 billion at year-end 2025 to $15.6 billion at June 27, 2026, and the $12.5 billion increase was booked as a loss "driven by an increase in our stock price" [10-Q Q1 2026, Note 4; 10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. No cash leaves; the real cost to shareholders is dilution (each existing share owning a smaller slice of the company), capped the day the deal was signed at 159 million shares [10-K FY2025, Note 5]. The loss reverses if the share price falls, so GAAP earnings will swing with the stock until the remaining 143 million shares are released [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 4]. In December 2025 the SEC staff made Intel book Secure Enclave cash as equity raised rather than as a grant [10-K FY2025, Note 5]. Non-GAAP net income, which excludes this loss, was $2.2 billion, or $0.42 a share [Q2 2026 release].

Return on capital, plainly: Intel carries $105.7 billion of factories and equipment and earned an operating loss on them in FY2024 and FY2025; whether Q2's $1.8 billion of operating income is a spike or the new normal decides everything [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 1; 10-K FY2025, Item 8; Q2 2026 release].

## 5. Why customers don't leave

**The x86 software base.** Most PC and server software is written for x86, and its "established software ecosystem continues to provide interoperability and performance across diverse workloads" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Moving a PC line or server fleet to Arm-based chips (a rival chip design used by Apple and Qualcomm) means re-testing everything, so customers move slowly. The catch: AMD sells x86 chips too, and Intel admits "we have lost market share in recent years, including in both client and data center markets" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Apple's 2020 switch to its own Arm chips shows what a customer big enough to rewrite the software can do [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Sign of weakening: Intel's unit volume falling while the market does not.

**Being the supplier who has chips.** In 2026 demand exceeded supply, Intel raised prices, and customers signed long-term arrangements with $1.7 billion of deposits [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. That is loyalty bought by shortage. Sign of weakening: price gains and deposits fading in the 10-Q.

**Owning US factories.** Being the only US leading-edge chipmaker is, our inference, why the government bought in; for foundry customers it matters only if the nodes work, and so far "We have been unsuccessful to date in securing any significant external foundry customers for any of our nodes" [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 1A]. Sign of weakening: outside Foundry revenue stuck near Altera's purchases.

## 6. What could break it

Ranked from most to least plausible.

**1. Foundry keeps losing money and 14A finds no outside customer.** Without "a significant external foundry customer for Intel 14A", Intel says it "may pause or discontinue" next-generation nodes, its products would "become dependent on third-party foundries, particularly TSMC", and "over $100 billion" of plant, mostly foundry, would face write-downs (charges booked when assets are judged worth less than their recorded cost) [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. In Q2 2026 management "committed to completing development of Intel 14A", but expansion "will ultimately be dictated by the amount of committed demand" [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. Early warnings: Foundry's loss not shrinking, outside revenue stuck near Altera's purchases, the 14A dates (risk production, the first small runs before full volume, in late 2027; volume in 2028) slipping [Q2 2026 call, p.2]. Exposure: the whole profit pool, since Products' margins depend on Foundry's wafer costs.

**2. The AI-server shortage ends, or spending moves further from CPUs.** DCAI's quarterly operating income rose from $633 million to $2.5 billion in a year on prices up 48% during a shortage [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 2; Item 2]. Shortage prices fall when shortages end; Intel notes NVIDIA's "GPU systems have experienced the highest demand in the market" while hyperscalers design their own chips [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Early warnings: server prices in the 10-Q turning down; the DCAI margin falling from 40%. Exposure: DCAI was 51% of Intel Products' operating income in Q2 2026 (computed) [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 2].

**3. PC shrinkage and share loss to AMD and Arm.** CCPG earned 73% of Intel Products' FY2025 operating income (computed), and management expects PC units "down low double digits percent for all of 2026" [10-K FY2025, Note 3; Q2 2026 call, p.5]. Named competitors: AMD, Apple, Qualcomm, MediaTek [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Early warnings: client volume falling faster than the market; prices reversing.

**4. Three customers are 43% of revenue.** Customer A alone was 19% in each of FY2023–FY2025 and the three largest were 40%, 45% and 43%; the FY2023 10-K named them (Dell, Lenovo, HP), later 10-Ks do not, and they owed 47% of the money customers owed Intel (receivables) at December 27, 2025 [10-K FY2023, Note 3; 10-K FY2025, Note 3; Note 2]. The 10-Qs give no figure; this updates once a year [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. Early warning: one of them going Arm-first or AMD-first.

**5. War around Israel.** "A significant portion" of revenue comes from Intel 7 chips made in Israel at a plant uninsured against war; in March 2026 Iran published a target list "with Intel being near the top of that list", and Iranian strikes on Qatari gas fields caused a global shortage of helium, "essential to the semiconductor manufacturing process" [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. Intel 7 is expected to be "almost half" of processor production in 2026 [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Early warning: any disruption disclosed in the 10-Q.

**6. Three smaller ones.** Sales billed to China were 24% of FY2025 revenue (computed) and tighter export controls "led to additional sales reductions"; some of Intel's most advanced products are "exclusively manufactured by TSMC" or depend on TSMC parts, with "no long-term contract"; and a March 2026 shareholder suit calls the government agreement unlawful, which Intel warns could leave the transactions "unauthorized, void or voidable" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A; 10-Q Q2 2026, Note 14]. Early warnings: the next 10-K's region table; the litigation note; none in the filings for TSMC.

## 7. Who runs it and what they do with the cash

Lip-Bu Tan, 66, has been CEO since March 18, 2025, after 12 years running Cadence Design Systems, a chip-design software company, and two years on Intel's board [10-K FY2025, Item 10; DEF 14A 2026, CD&A]. He bought $25 million of shares on joining and must keep them for his hiring awards to vest (become his to keep) [DEF 14A 2026, CD&A]. Dave Zinsner has been CFO since January 2022 and, since April 24, 2026, also principal accounting officer (the executive who signs off on the books) after the chief accounting officer resigned [10-K FY2025, Item 10; 8-K 2026-04-24]. The chief legal officer left on June 1, 2026 [8-K 2026-04-03]. Craig Barratt, a director since 2025, replaced Frank Yeary as independent chair at the May 13, 2026 meeting [8-K 2026-03-03; DEF 14A 2026, Director Nominees]. Intel lists its "CEO transitions in 2025, 2024, 2021 and 2019" as a risk [10-K FY2025, Item 1A].

Track record. The 2021–2024 build-out produced four years of negative adjusted free cash flow and an $18.8 billion loss in FY2024, including a $9.9 billion tax charge and $3.3 billion of Intel 7 write-downs [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. Tan's first year cut headcount from 108,900 to 85,100, raised $12.7 billion by selling shares, sold 51% of Altera for $4.3 billion net at a $5.6 billion gain, wrote down $3.9 billion of Mobileye goodwill (the premium paid over a bought company's asset value, carried on the balance sheet) in Q1 2026, then spent $14.2 billion buying back Apollo's 49% of the Irish fab, charging $13.5 billion to equity [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 7; Item 8; Note 10; 10-Q Q1 2026, Note 10; 10-Q Q2 2026, Note 3].

Pay tracks this report's numbers: the 2025 cash bonus weighed revenue, gross-margin percentage, operating expense, "Intel Top Jobs" goals and individual goals at 20% each (25% each of the first four for the CEO), and Tan agreed to a payout cut from 123.4% to 118.7% of target to match employees [DEF 14A 2026, CD&A]. His 2025 stock awards vest on three-year shareholder return against the S&P 500 (an index of 500 large US companies), and his one-time hiring awards pay nothing unless the share price rises [DEF 14A 2026, CD&A]. Reported 2025 pay was $93.0 million, $42 million of it hiring awards; support in the say-on-pay vote (shareholders' advisory vote on executive pay) was 72% in 2025 and 86.9% of votes for and against in 2026 (computed; 86.5% counting abstentions) [DEF 14A 2026, CD&A; 8-K 2026-05-15].

Ownership. The US government holds 8.4% (433.3 million shares, counting the escrowed ones) and "has agreed to vote its shares of common stock as recommended by our board of directors"; Vanguard holds 8.1%, BlackRock 6.8%, and all directors and officers together "not more than 1%" [DEF 14A 2026, Security Ownership; 10-K FY2025, Item 1A].

## Glossary

- **Foundry** — a factory business that manufactures chips designed by others; Intel Foundry makes Intel's own chips today and wants outside customers.
- **Wafer** — the thin disc of silicon on which hundreds of chips are made at once; foundries sell by the wafer.
- **Process node** — one generation of manufacturing technology (Intel 7, Intel 3, Intel 18A, Intel 14A); each new node packs in more transistors and costs more to develop.
- **x86** — the set of basic instructions (the "language") that Intel's and AMD's CPUs understand; most PC and server software is written for it.
- **GAAP / non-GAAP** — GAAP is the official accounting standard; Intel's non-GAAP figures remove stock pay, acquisition amortization, restructuring and the escrowed-shares loss.
- **Capex** — capital expenditure; cash spent on factories, machines and other long-lived assets.
- **Amortization** — spreading the price paid for acquired technology and customer relationships over several years as a yearly expense; Intel's non-GAAP figures remove it.
- **Non-controlling interest (NCI)** — the share of a consolidated subsidiary's profit or equity that belongs to an outside owner, here Brookfield's 49% of the Arizona fabs and minority holders of Mobileye.
- **Derivative liability** — an obligation whose value moves with something else; Intel's promise to release escrowed shares is valued at their current market price and re-measured each quarter.
- **Advanced packaging / EMIB** — joining several chips ("tiles") into one working part; EMIB and EMIB-T are Intel's methods, sold to outside customers as a foundry service.
