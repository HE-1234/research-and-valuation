# Micron Technology, Inc. — The Business
_As of Q3 FY2026 (quarter ended May 28, 2026). Written 2026-09-07._

Micron's fiscal year ends on the Thursday closest to August 31; FY2025 ended August 28, 2025, and FY2026 ends September 3, 2026, a 53-week year whose fourth quarter "contains 14 weeks" [10-K FY2025, Item 7; 8-K 2026-01-21; 10-Q Q3 FY2026, Note 1].

## 1. What they do

Micron makes memory chips: the parts of a computer, phone, car or AI server that hold data while it is being worked on (DRAM) or keep it when the power is off (NAND) [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. It designs the chips, makes them in its own factories ("fabs") in Taiwan, Singapore, Japan and the United States, and assembles and tests them mostly in Asia [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 2]. Customers are the companies that build the machines: data-center operators, server, PC and smartphone makers, and car and industrial-equipment makers, plus a small consumer line under the Crucial brand [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. DRAM was 76% of revenue in FY2025 and NAND 23% (computed from the revenue-by-technology table) [10-K FY2025, Note 21].

The first thing to understand is that memory is close to a commodity. A gigabit of DRAM from Micron does the same job as one from Samsung or SK hynix, so customers buy on price and availability, and the price is set by industry supply against world demand [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 1A]. Customers "are generally reluctant to enter into long-term, fixed-price purchase contracts", and terms are "periodically negotiated to reflect market conditions" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Supply cannot answer quickly: the Idaho fab broke ground in September 2022 and is now expected to produce its first wafers in mid-2027, so when demand jumps prices spike, and when it falls prices crash [10-K FY2025, Item 2; 10-Q Q3 FY2026, Item 2]. Over the past five years the yearly change in DRAM prices has ranged "from plus low 40% to a minus high 40% range" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A].

What makes the present unusual is AI. AI servers need far more memory each, including a stacked form of DRAM called HBM, and demand has grown "at a rate greater than our ability and the industry's ability to increase supply" [10-Q Q3 FY2026, Item 2]. Micron's gross margin (the share of each sales dollar left after paying to make the product) reached 85% in Q3 FY2026, three years after a year in which it sold memory for less than it cost to make [10-Q Q3 FY2026, Item 2; 10-K FY2023, Item 7].

History, as far as the filings tell it (the FY2025 10-K has no founding paragraph): Sanjay Mehrotra became CEO in May 2017 [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. In 2021 Micron abandoned a third memory type, 3D XPoint, and sold its Lehi, Utah fab to Texas Instruments in 2022 for $893 million [10-K FY2023, Item 7]. In 2023 China's cybersecurity regulator barred operators of "critical information infrastructure" from buying Micron products, and a price collapse forced a roughly 15% headcount cut [10-K FY2025, Item 7; Note 22]. In December 2024 it signed grant agreements under the CHIPS Act (the 2022 US law that subsidises domestic chip factories) for US fabs, and in FY2025 it reorganized into four customer-focused business units [10-K FY2025, Note 20; Note 27].

## 2. How the money comes in

Micron sells chips and nothing else of any size, directly and through distributors who also sell "our competitors' products"; the distributor share is not disclosed [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. About half of revenue comes from the top ten customers, one customer was 17% of FY2025 revenue, and about half of FY2025 revenue went to the data-center market [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Note 28; Item 1A].

The consistent five-year view is by technology; NOR is an older memory that stores code in cars and industrial gear [10-K FY2025, Item 1].

The shift in that table is the story: Cloud Memory grew 257% in FY2025, "driven by accelerating AI demand in cloud server markets for HBM" and high-capacity server modules, while Mobile and Client barely grew because its "product supply was constrained to meet demand from higher-value segments" [10-K FY2025, Item 7].

A new way of getting paid appeared this quarter: "strategic customer agreements" (SCAs), multi-year take-or-pay contracts, typically calendar 2026 through 2030, in which customers commit to set volumes, mostly at fixed prices or within a floor and ceiling, and put down cash deposits [10-Q Q3 FY2026, Note 14; Q3 FY2026 remarks, p.3]. Management expects "approximately half or more of our company revenue" to be under SCAs when the program is complete [Q3 FY2026 remarks, p.3].

## 3. The economics

Costs are mostly fixed: "A significant portion of our manufacturing costs are fixed and do not vary proportionally with changes in production output" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Depreciation (the yearly accounting charge that spreads a fab's cost over its life) alone was $8.28 billion in FY2025 [10-K FY2025, Note 8]. Cost per bit falls with each new node, but slowly, while prices move by tens of percent a year [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 1A]. So gross margin moves almost one for one with price.

Our inference: the factories, nodes and product mix changed far less between those columns than the price of what came out of them.

Micron's "non-GAAP" figures strip out stock-based pay and debt-prepayment losses; the gap is small (84.9% versus 84.6% gross margin in Q3 FY2026), so this report uses GAAP except where guidance is non-GAAP [Q3 FY2026 release].

Capital intensity is the other half. Over FY2021–FY2025 capex was $54.0 billion against $136.5 billion of revenue, about 40% (computed), and FY2026 is guided to "approximately $27 billion" net of government incentives [10-K FY2023, Item 8; 10-K FY2025, Item 8; 10-Q Q3 FY2026, Item 2]. The filings do not split "stay in place" from "grow", but describe both [10-K FY2025, Item 7; 10-Q Q3 FY2026, Item 2]. Staying in place means node transitions (1-gamma DRAM, G9 NAND, Micron's ninth-generation NAND, and EUV lithography tools, the newest chip-patterning machines) and "tool replacements and upgrades to improve productivity" [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Q3 FY2026 remarks, p.6]. Growing means new cleanrooms (the ultra-clean factory floors where wafers are processed): two Idaho fabs (first wafers mid-2027 and late 2028), a New York site for "supply in 2030 and beyond", the $1.8 billion Tongluo fab bought in Taiwan in March 2026, and expansions in Singapore and Japan [10-Q Q3 FY2026, Item 2; Note 7].

Governments pay part of the bill: CHIPS Act grants of up to $6.4 billion, paid only "based on the achievement of construction, tool installation, and wafer production milestones" and subject to clawback; a US investment tax credit raised from 25% to 35% in July 2025; up to about $3.4 billion from Japan for Hiroshima; and 70% of the Gujarat plant's cost from India (50% from the central government plus 20% from the state, computed) [10-K FY2025, Note 20].

## 4. How profitable, really

Over the five years to FY2025 the answer, in cash, was: barely. Operating cash flow totalled $55.2 billion and capex $54.0 billion (computed); the business turned almost every dollar back into fabs, and free cash flow after government incentives was $4.9 billion for the whole period (computed) [10-K FY2023, Item 8; 10-K FY2025, Item 8]. Nine months of FY2026 then produced $29.1 billion (computed), six times the five-year total [10-Q Q3 FY2026, Item 1].

And $5.79 billion of income taxes payable now sits in noncurrent liabilities because a 15% global minimum tax largely offsets Micron's Singapore tax break [10-Q Q3 FY2026, Note 15].

## 5. Why customers don't leave

For most of Micron's history the honest answer was that they leave whenever someone else is cheaper: contracts were renegotiated "to reflect market conditions", and the company's stated ambition is "maintaining stable bit share" (market share measured in bits) [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 2]. The moat, such as it is, has three sources.

**Being one of very few who can make it.** Leading-edge memory needs fabs that take years to build, over 60,000 granted patents and decades of process know-how; the FY2025 10-K names six competitors, two of them (CXMT and YMTC) Chinese state-backed entrants that were not on the FY2023 10-K's list [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 2; 10-K FY2023, Item 1]. Sign of weakening: Chinese makers named in the MD&A's price commentary.

**Qualification on complex products.** HBM, data-center SSDs and managed NAND (NAND packaged with its own controller chip) must be designed in and qualified (tested and approved) by each customer, which makes switching slow and costly; "If we do not meet their product design schedules, our customers may exclude us from further consideration as a supplier for those products" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Sign of weakening: Cloud Memory revenue or gross margin falling faster than the company's.

**The new lock-in: SCAs.** The 16 agreements are "take-or-pay agreements, with binding commitments to purchase specific volumes", covering "roughly 20% of our DRAM volume and a third of our NAND volume" [Q3 FY2026 remarks, p.3]. Fourteen carry "a cumulative revenue at minimum price per our contracts of approximately $100 billion" (a figure that includes agreements signed after May 28, 2026; quarter-end RPO was about $5 billion), backed by "$22 billion" of deposits and commitments, "approximately $18 billion" in cash [Q3 FY2026 remarks, p.3; p.7; 10-Q Q3 FY2026, Note 14]. That is a real switching cost: a signed customer cannot walk away without paying for volume it did not take. But note the cause: "In a period of significant shortage, this supply visibility is extremely beneficial to our customers" [Q3 FY2026 remarks, p.3]. Customers signed because they could not get memory, not because Micron's memory is different; our inference is that these contracts measure the shortage as much as loyalty. Sign of weakening: signings stopping short of "half or more", RPO falling, or floors renegotiated when market prices drop below them.

## 6. What could break it

Ranked from most to least plausible.

**1. The cycle turns.** What has to happen: supply catches up (management expects "industry supply to improve gradually in 2028"), or demand air-pockets, including the 10-Q's new warning that high prices may lead customers to "reduce memory and storage content" [Q3 FY2026 remarks, p.2; 10-Q Q3 FY2026, Part II Item 1A]. Or HBM demand weakens and "suppliers shift capacity from HBM to conventional DRAM" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. FY2023 is the worked example: revenue down 49%, gross margin negative 9%, a $1,831 million inventory write-down, a $5,745 million operating loss, the bonus suspended and about 15% of staff cut, all within a year of a 45%-margin year [10-K FY2023, Item 7; 10-K FY2025, Note 22]. Exposed profit pool: all of it. Early warning: the price direction in the 10-Q, inventory days rising, capex rising while prices flatten; note management's own phrase this quarter, "a meaningful moderation in the rate of price increases" [Q3 FY2026 remarks, p.10].

**2. Chinese competitors and Chinese policy.** CXMT (DRAM) and YMTC (NAND) are state-backed, and the 10-K warns of "oversupply due to significant investment in the semiconductor industry, including by the Chinese government" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. China's 2023 ban already bit: revenue from customers headquartered in mainland China and Hong Kong fell from about 16% of revenue in FY2022–FY2024 to 10% in FY2025 (computed) [10-K FY2025, Note 29; 10-K FY2023, Item 8]. Exposed: China revenue now, commodity prices later. Early warning: CXMT or YMTC named in the MD&A's price commentary.

**3. HBM competition and a few AI customers.** One customer was 17% of FY2025 revenue, "primarily included in the CMBU segment" (Cloud Memory), and "over half" came from the top ten [10-K FY2025, Note 28; Item 1A]. Samsung and SK hynix compete for the same customers, and HBM requires "achieving acceptable yields and quality" (yield: the share of chips made that actually work) across stacked layers [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Exposed: the 61% of revenue in the two data-center units (computed). Early warning: the 10%-customer share in the 10-Q, Cloud Memory margin versus the other units, HBM4 qualification news.

**4. Taiwan.** "A majority of our DRAM production output in 2025 was from our fabrication facilities in Taiwan", where $19.0 billion of $47.3 billion of long-lived assets sit [10-K FY2025, Item 1A; Note 29]. Exposed: most DRAM output. The filings offer no early warning; the Idaho, Japan and Singapore builds are the mitigation, years away.

**5. Export controls and tariffs.** About 80% of revenue ships outside the United States; US restrictions on some customers "may not prohibit our competitors from selling similar products to our customers"; and a Section 232 investigation "may result in industry-wide additional tariffs and trade restrictions" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Early warning: new Commerce Department rules; changed 10-Q risk wording.

**6. Fab execution and CHIPS conditions.** Idaho's first output has already moved from "early calendar 2026" to "mid-calendar 2027" [10-K FY2023, Item 7; 10-Q Q3 FY2026, Item 2]. Grants depend on milestones, with "up to all of certain incentives being clawed back" on failure, and the agreements restrict special and one-time dividends for five years from December 9, 2024, cap buybacks at specified amounts during the first two of those years, and free them in the final three only "if certain financial and other conditions are satisfied" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A; Note 20]. Exposed: the roughly $27 billion a year of capex. Early warning: milestone dates slipping in the 10-Q.

**7. SCA counterparty risk.** The contracts are untested in a downturn. If prices fall below the floors, customers pay above market, and "if customers fail to meet their purchase commitments, we may need to enforce our contractual rights, which could result in litigation or disputes" [10-Q Q3 FY2026, Part II Item 1A]. Deposits are not Micron's to keep: "This cash will be returned to customers over time, towards the latter half of the agreement term" [Q3 FY2026 remarks, p.7]. The agreements could "constrain our available supply and limit our flexibility to respond to changes in market conditions", and the ceiling at calendar-Q2-2026 prices caps the upside if prices keep rising [10-Q Q3 FY2026, Part II Item 1A; Item 2]. Early warning: RPO falling without matching revenue; disputes in the contingencies note (the 10-Q's note on lawsuits and disputes).

## 7. Who runs it and what they do with the cash

Sanjay Mehrotra, 67, who co-founded SanDisk in 1988 and ran it until its 2016 sale, "has served as Micron's President, Chief Executive Officer, and Director since May 2017 and has served as Micron's Chairman since January 2025" [10-K FY2025, Item 1; DEF 14A 2025, Board of Directors]. CFO Mark Murphy joined in April 2022 after six years as Qorvo's CFO [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Seven of the eight directors elected in January 2026 are independent, including T. Mark Liu, former Executive Chairman of TSMC, and Robert Swan, former Intel CEO, with Lynn Dugle as Lead Independent Director; Alexis Black Björlin, formerly of NVIDIA, joined in June 2026 [DEF 14A 2025, Proposal 1; Board Structure; 8-K 2026-01-21; 8-K 2026-06-09]. Insiders own less than 1%; Vanguard (8.4%), BlackRock (7.6%) and Capital World (6.3%) are the largest holders [DEF 14A 2025, Security Ownership].

Pay follows the cycle. The CEO's FY2025 pay was $30.9 million, $25.4 million of it stock; the cash bonus is half profit (non-GAAP net income or operating margin) and half strategic goals, which in FY2025 included HBM3E yield and "Days of inventory outstanding"; three-year performance shares pay on HBM and data-center SSD share and on shareholder return against the SOX chip index [DEF 14A 2025, Summary Compensation Table; CD&A]. In the downturn the bonus "was suspended in February 2023 and no amounts were paid" [DEF 14A 2025, Summary Compensation Table].

Track record: 3D XPoint was abandoned after a $435 million charge rather than funded further; the 2023 downturn was met by cutting capex 36% (computed) and headcount 15%; the FY2025 shift of wafers to the data center worked; against that, the Idaho fab is more than a year late (section 6) [10-K FY2023, Item 7; 10-K FY2025, Item 7; Note 22].

## Glossary

- **DRAM** — dynamic random access memory; the fast working memory a processor uses while running, which loses its contents when power is off; about three-quarters of Micron's revenue.
- **NAND** — flash memory that keeps data without power; the storage inside SSDs, phones and cars.
- **HBM** — high-bandwidth memory; several DRAM chips stacked and wired vertically so an AI processor can read them far faster; sold mainly for AI servers.
- **Bit** — the smallest unit of memory; the industry measures output and prices per bit because chips of different sizes are otherwise not comparable.
- **Node** — a generation of manufacturing technology (Micron's current DRAM node is "1-gamma"); each new node fits more bits on a wafer and lowers cost per bit.
- **Fab** — a chip factory, where wafers are processed into chips; a new one takes years to build.
- **Wafer** — the thin disc of silicon (300 mm across at Micron) on which hundreds of chips are made at once before being cut apart.
- **Take-or-pay** — a contract in which the buyer must pay for the agreed volume whether or not it takes delivery.
- **RPO** — remaining performance obligations; the revenue still owed to Micron under signed contracts, counted here at minimum volumes and minimum prices.
- **Gross margin / operating margin** — gross margin is the share of each sales dollar left after the cost of making the product; operating margin is what is left after research, selling and administrative costs as well.
- **SSD** — solid-state drive; a storage device built from NAND chips plus a controller chip, replacing spinning hard disks in servers and PCs.
- **10-K / 10-Q** — the annual (10-K) and quarterly (10-Q) reports every US-listed company must file with the SEC, the US securities regulator; the primary sources for this report.
- **GAAP / non-GAAP** — GAAP is the official accounting standard; Micron's "non-GAAP" figures remove stock-based pay, debt-prepayment losses and one-off items.
- **Capex** — capital expenditure; cash spent on fabs, tools and buildings rather than on day-to-day costs.
