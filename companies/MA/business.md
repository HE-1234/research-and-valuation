# Mastercard Incorporated — The Business
_As of Q2 2026. Written 2026-09-08._

## 1. What they do

Mastercard runs one of the two large global card payment networks. When someone pays with a Mastercard-branded card, the shop's bank sends the transaction to Mastercard, which passes it to the cardholder's bank for approval and then moves the money between the two banks; the company's term is to "switch (authorize, clear and settle) payment transactions" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. The filings call this a "four-party" network of account holder, issuer ("the account holder's financial institution"), merchant and acquirer ("the merchant's financial institution") [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Mastercard owns the brand, the rulebook and the plumbing in the middle, but it is not a bank: "We do not issue cards, extend credit, determine or receive revenue from interest rates or other fees charged to account holders by issuers" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Its paying customers are mostly the banks on both sides [10-K FY2025, Item 1A].

The scale is huge and the fee per transaction tiny. In 2025, $10.6 trillion ran over Mastercard-branded cards in more than 220 countries and territories and 175.5 billion transactions were switched; 3.7 billion Mastercard and Maestro (its PIN-debit brand) cards were in issue at June 30, 2026 [10-K FY2025, Item 1] [Q2 2026 release, p.2]. Mastercard itself switches "more than 70% of all transactions for Mastercard and Maestro-branded cards, including nearly all cross-border transactions"; the rest run over domestic systems carrying its brand [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. It also sells fraud, data and consulting services to the same banks and runs bank-to-bank payment systems such as Vocalink in the U.K. [10-K FY2025, Item 1].

History. Mastercard International was formed in November 1966 as a body owned by its member banks; the parent was incorporated in Delaware in May 2001 and listed in May 2006, when customer banks received non-voting Class B shares [10-K FY2025, Item 1] [10-K FY2025, Note 19] [DEF 14A 2026, p.115]. The Mastercard Foundation, a charity independent of the company, still holds 7.4% (§7) [DEF 14A 2026, p.112]. Michael Miebach has been CEO since January 2021 [10-K FY2025, Part I, Executive Officers].

The take is thin: gross assessments were about 0.38% of 2025 GDV and payment network net revenue about 0.18% (computed; GDV includes volume Mastercard does not itself switch) [10-K FY2025, Item 1] [10-K FY2025, Item 7].

**Interchange is not Mastercard's money.** The largest fee in a card payment is interchange, "collected from acquirers and paid to issuers to reimburse the issuers for a portion of the costs incurred"; Mastercard sets the default rates but "we do not earn revenues from interchange" [10-K FY2025, Item 1] [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. It still matters: if rates are too high "merchants may stop accepting our products or route transactions away from our network", and if too low "issuers may stop promoting our products" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Nearly all the lawsuits and regulation aimed at Mastercard are about interchange (§6).

**Rebates and incentives.** To win and keep a bank's card business, Mastercard pays part of its fees back: fixed incentives "directly related to entering into an agreement", spread over contracts of "generally up to 10 years", and variable rebates tied to volume [10-K FY2025, Note 1] [10-K FY2025, Note 3]. They have grown faster than the fees: from 2021 to 2025 gross assessments rose 78%, rebates 96% and payment network net revenue 63% (computed) [10-K FY2023, Item 7] [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. In Q2 2026 rebates rose 22%, "primarily due to an increase in our key drivers, as well as new and renewed deals" [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2].

**Value-added services and solutions (41% of 2025 revenue).** Fees, "either fixed or transaction-based", for seven product families [10-K FY2025, Note 3]: in plain words, fraud and cyber tools, loyalty and marketing programmes, consulting and data, identity checks, payment processing, bank-to-bank systems, and open finance (letting customers share their bank data with apps). The line grew 23% in 2025 and 18% currency-neutral in Q2 2026; no dollar split inside it is disclosed [10-K FY2025, Item 7] [Q2 2026 release, p.2] [10-K FY2025, Note 3]. Management says "about 60%" of it is tied to network transactions (a transcript-only figure) [Q2 2026 call, prepared remarks, Miebach].

**Where and from whom.** The U.S. was 29% of 2025 net revenue; the five largest customers, unnamed, were "approximately $6.9 billion, or 21%, of total net revenue" [10-K FY2025, Note 22] [10-K FY2025, Item 7].

## 5. Why customers don't leave

**Two-sided network.** A card is worth carrying because it works everywhere, and a shop accepts it because everyone carries one. Mastercard's own list of advantages starts with "Global network" and a "Franchise model" of "Establishing rules, standards and bearing of financial risk" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Only Visa, American Express, JCB, China UnionPay and Discover are named as global rivals [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Against local networks the CEO cites "our global acceptance reach, consumer protections, digital capabilities and security overlays" [Q2 2026 call, prepared remarks, Miebach].

**Contracts and switching cost.** Banks sign deals of up to ten years with incentives spread over the term, so leaving forfeits promised payments, and moving a portfolio means reissuing every card [10-K FY2025, Note 3] [10-K FY2025, Note 1]. The live example is the Capital One debit portfolio that left Mastercard: its migration "was basically complete in Q1" 2026, and in Q2 2026 U.S. debit volume grew 1.3% while U.S. credit grew 9.5% [Q2 2026 call, prepared remarks, Mehra] [Q2 2026 release, p.13].

**Services and discipline.** Fraud tools, tokens and data are sold on top of the switch, about 60% of services revenue being "network linked" (transcript-only figure) [Q2 2026 call, prepared remarks, Miebach]. The company says it declines deals that do not pay: "Volume for the sake of volume is not what this company should be chasing" [Q2 2026 call, Q&A, Harshita Rawat].

**Brand.** "Priceless" has run for nearly 30 years in more than 120 countries, on under 3% of revenue in advertising (computed) [10-K FY2025, Item 1] [10-K FY2025, Item 8].

**Signs of weakening.** The switched share ("more than 70%") falling; rebates rising as a share of gross assessments faster than the roughly one point a year of 2023–2025 (1.4 then 0.9 points); switched transactions growing slower than GDV, meaning domestic systems are taking the processing; another named portfolio leaving [10-K FY2025, Item 1] [10-K FY2025, Item 7].

## 6. What could break it

Ranked by plausibility. The company's own summary: rulings and laws on "interchange fees and acceptance practices may have a material adverse effect on the Company's prospects for future growth" [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 14].

**1. Governments cap interchange or network fees.** The U.S. already caps debit interchange under Regulation II; in 2025 two federal courts issued "conflicting decisions regarding the validity of the interchange cap", which "remains in effect while the litigation continues" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. The 10-K names "proposed legislation in the U.S. to extend routing mandates to credit" (letting merchants send credit transactions over a cheaper network) and "the EU regulation capping consumer credit and debit interchange fees" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. The next step would be Mastercard's own fees: "debit regulations could lead to regulation of credit products", and "Some merchants are increasingly asking regulators to review and potentially regulate our own network fees" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Early warning: a U.S. credit-routing bill reaching a vote; a network-fee review; rebates jumping as Mastercard compensates banks. Exposure: the U.S. is 29% of revenue, and every payment network dollar rests on pricing freedom [10-K FY2025, Note 22].

**2. Litigation.** The main U.S. merchant case, running since 2005, is being settled: Mastercard and Visa agreed a "10 basis point reduction in the average systemwide effective interchange rate" on U.S. credit (0.1 percentage point), a "Five-year rate cap" and looser surcharging (adding a fee for paying by card) and acceptance rules; the court "granted preliminary approval of the settlement in June 2026" and "A hearing on final approval is scheduled for November 2026" [8-K 2025-11-10] [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 14]. Mastercard accrued $149 million for the U.S. cases at June 30, 2026, down from $637 million after payments, but still faces opt-out merchants (those who stayed outside the class settlement to sue on their own) seeking "in excess of $250 million" at a September 2026 trial and Block and Intuit seeking "in excess of $5 billion" [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 14]. Abroad: "over £0.5 billion" of unresolved U.K. merchant claims, a U.K. commercial-card action "in excess of £1 billion", and smaller Portuguese, Dutch and ATM-surcharge cases [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 14]. Total accrued litigation (money set aside for expected legal payments) was $296 million at June 30, 2026 [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 1]. Note: the FY2025 10-K described a 2023 Department of Justice Civil Investigative Demand (a formal demand for documents in an antitrust investigation) into "Mastercard's U.S. debit program and competition with other payment networks and technologies"; neither 2026 10-Q legal note mentions the DOJ, and the filings do not say why [10-K FY2025, Note 19] [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 14]. Early warning: the provision line and the accrued balance; a settlement rejected or a trial lost. Exposure: antitrust (competition-law) plaintiffs can claim treble (triple) damages [10-K FY2025, Item 1A].

**3. Domestic and government payment systems take the transactions.** "In various countries, local debit brands serve as the main domestic brands", "several governments are promoting, or considering promoting, local networks for domestic switching", and government real-time systems (the 10-K names Brazil's PIX, FedNow in the U.S. and India's UPI) are "increasingly being considered as alternatives to traditional domestic payment solutions and schemes such as ours" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Mastercard's answer is to run the domestic switch itself where it can; a UAE central-bank partnership was announced on the Q2 call [Q2 2026 call, prepared remarks, Miebach]. Early warning: switched transactions growing slower than GDV; Europe slowing further (Europe GDV grew 8.5% in Q2 2026 against 13.3% a year earlier) [Q2 2026 release, p.13] [Q2 2026 release, p.14]. Exposure: transaction processing ($15.9 billion gross in 2025) and domestic assessments ($11.0 billion) [10-K FY2025, Item 7].

**4. A large bank or merchant defects.** The five largest customers are 21% of revenue, "Many of our customer relationships are not exclusive", and bank mergers have moved Mastercard portfolios to banks close to a competitor [10-K FY2025, Item 7] [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Early warning: the five-largest share moving; U.S. debit growth (1.3% in Q2 2026); rebates jumping with "new and renewed deals" [Q2 2026 release, p.13] [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. Exposure: a top-five customer averages about 4% of revenue (computed).

**5. A cyber breach or outage.** Mastercard is a designated systemically important payment system in the EU, and at Vocalink "Any prolonged service outage on this network could result in quickly escalating impacts, including potential intervention by the Bank of England" [10-K FY2025, Item 1] [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Early warning: an incident disclosed in a 10-Q or 8-K. Exposure: trust is the product; no dollar figure is disclosed.

**Other exposures.** The settlement guarantee ($93.4 billion gross) if several banks fail at once, widened by a November 2025 Brazilian rule [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 15] [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Tax: the effective rate rose from 15.6% to 19.4% in 2025 as the 15% global minimum tax "largely offsets" a Singapore incentive [10-K FY2025, Item 7] [10-K FY2025, Note 18].

**People.** Michael Miebach, 58, has been CEO since January 2021 and Sachin Mehra, 55, CFO since April 2019 [10-K FY2025, Part I, Executive Officers]. On 2026-06-02 the company announced that, effective 2026-08-03, Mehra "will assume the role of Chief Business Officer", Ling Hai, 56, "will be appointed to succeed Mr. Mehra as Chief Financial Officer", Linda Kirkpatrick becomes Chief Services Officer, Craig Vosburg Vice Chair, and Timothy Murphy "will retire from Mastercard in October 2026" [8-K 2026-06-02]. ## Glossary

- **Issuer / acquirer:** the cardholder's bank (issuer) and the merchant's bank (acquirer); both are Mastercard's customers.
- **Interchange:** the fee the merchant's bank pays the cardholder's bank on each card payment; Mastercard sets the default rate but does not receive it.
- **Assessments:** Mastercard's own fees to banks, per dollar of volume and per transaction, before rebates are deducted.
- **Rebates and incentives:** payments Mastercard makes back to banks and some merchants to win and keep their business, deducted from revenue.
- **Gross dollar volume (GDV):** the total dollars of purchases and cash withdrawals on Mastercard-branded cards, including transactions Mastercard did not itself switch.
- **Switched transaction:** a transaction that Mastercard's own network authorised, cleared and settled.
- **Cross-border volume:** spending where the card was issued in one country and the merchant is in another; it has its own assessment line.
- **Token (tokenization):** a substitute number that stands in for the real card number in a phone or online wallet, so the card number is never exposed.
- **Currency-neutral:** a growth rate calculated as if exchange rates had not moved.
- **GAAP / adjusted:** GAAP is the standard U.S. accounting rulebook; "adjusted" (non-GAAP) figures strip out litigation charges, one-off items and investment gains or losses.
