# Rocket Lab Corporation — The Business
_As of Q2 2026. Written 2026-09-07._

## 1. What they do

Rocket Lab builds and launches small rockets, and builds satellites and the parts inside them, for governments and companies that want something in orbit [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. It calls itself "an end-to-end space company" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. **Launch Services**, one of two reported segments, is the Electron rocket, which carries up to 300 kg to low Earth orbit and had flown 87 successful missions by June 30, 2026; HASTE, an Electron variant that flies suborbital test flights for "hypersonic and suborbital system technology development", including missile-defence testing for the Space Force (hypersonic: over five times the speed of sound; suborbital: see Glossary); and Neutron, a much larger reusable rocket still in development (§3) [10-K FY2025, Item 1; 10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. **Space Systems** is everything that is not a rocket: satellite platforms (called buses) built to order, plus components sold to other satellite makers, such as solar cells, star trackers (cameras that tell a satellite which way it points), reaction wheels (spinning wheels that turn a satellite without fuel), radios, separation systems, flight software, laser communication terminals (Mynaric, 2026) and infrared sensors (GEOST, 2025) [10-K FY2025, Item 1; 10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2].

Who pays? For launch, "government customers, such as the United States Department of War ("DoW"), NASA and other U.S. government agencies", plus commercial satellite operators such as Planet and BlackSky [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. About 47% of FY2025 revenue was U.S. government-related (§2) [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Management's explanation of why customers pay: "Launch has never been so constrained" (§5) [Q2 2026 call].

History. Peter Beck founded the company in New Zealand in 2006; Electron first flew in 2017 from Launch Complex 1 on the Mahia Peninsula, which may launch U.S. rocket technology under a U.S.–New Zealand treaty [DEF 14A 2026, Proposal One; 10-K FY2025, Item 1]. It listed on August 25, 2021 through a merger with Vector Acquisition Corporation, bought four component makers in 2020–2022 (Sinclair Interplanetary, Advanced Solutions, Planetary Systems, SolAero), reorganized under a new parent, Rocket Lab Corporation, in May 2025, and then bought GEOST (2025), Mynaric and Motiv (2026) [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Note 4; 10-K FY2023, Note 4; 10-Q Q2 2026, Note 4]. On June 28, 2026 it agreed to buy Iridium Communications, operator of a 66-satellite voice-and-data network, for $54 per share in cash and stock, an enterprise value (share price plus debt) of about $8.0 billion; the deal is pending, needs Iridium shareholder and regulatory approvals, and "is expected to close in 2027" [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 1; Item 2; 8-K 2026-06-29]. If it closes, Rocket Lab would for the first time own a subscription business ($871 million of 2025 revenue, on Iridium's numbers) [Iridium deck 2026-06-29, p.6].

## 2. How the money comes in

There are three kinds of sale [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. A **launch contract** is a fixed price for placing a satellite in orbit; milestone cash sits as a deposit and becomes revenue only at lift-off (HASTE, booked as the vehicle is built, is the exception), so launch revenue jumps around [10-K FY2025, Item 7; Note 2; 10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. A **spacecraft contract** is a multi-year fixed-price build; revenue is recognized "over time", in proportion to costs incurred against the estimated total, so estimates matter and can be revised mid-contract [10-K FY2025, Item 7; Note 2]. A **component sale** is a purchase order booked when the part ships [10-K FY2025, Item 7].

## 3. The economics

Both businesses are factories with heavy fixed costs. Pads, production lines and mission control cost about the same whether six or twenty rockets fly, so "increasing our production rate resulting in greater absorption of these costs is our most critical cost reduction initiative" [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. It has worked for Electron: launches rose from 6 in 2021 to 21 in 2025, cost per launch fell from $9.2 million to $4.8 million while revenue per launch rose from $8.1 million to $8.5 million, turning a business that lost money on every flight into one with about a 41% gross margin (revenue left after direct costs, as a share of revenue) (computed) [10-K FY2023, Item 7; 10-K FY2025, Item 7; Note 20]. Space Systems margins depend on mix: some off-the-shelf components earn "north of 70 points of gross margin", while the big fixed-price Space Development Agency (SDA) Tranche 2 and 3 satellite builds sit "more kind of in the mid-30s" (our reading: gross margin), so the blended margin can fall while revenue rises, as guided for Q3 2026 [Q2 2026 call; Q2 2026 release].

**Why it is not yet profitable.** Gross profit does not cover R&D plus SG&A. In FY2025 gross profit was $207.2 million against $270.7 million of R&D and $165.3 million of SG&A, an operating loss of $228.8 million [10-K FY2025, Item 8]. In Q2 2026 gross profit of $84.6 million almost matched R&D of $82.4 million, and the $59.7 million of SG&A was the loss [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 1]. Adjusted EBITDA, the company's own profit measure that removes interest, tax, depreciation, stock-based pay and deal costs, was a loss of only $8.8 million in Q2 2026, because $20.9 million of depreciation and amortization (computed), $19.6 million of stock pay and $8.6 million of deal costs are added back [Q2 2026 release]. R&D is the swing item, and the 10-K attributes each year's increase "primarily due to Neutron development progress" [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. Management's stated path: the first successful Neutron launch "will be the real turning point where we go to adjusted EBITDA positivity in the quarter after that, that event happens", and "it's going to probably be 18 to 24 months after that pivot that we get to cash flow positivity because we'll have to continue to invest in a fleet of tails to build out for Neutrons" (tails: individual rockets) [Q2 2026 call]. The filings give no date: "We expect to continue to incur net losses for at least the next 12 months" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A].

Capital intensity is high: capex (cash spent on equipment, buildings and launch sites) was 26% of revenue in FY2025, and the CFO expects it "to remain elevated" [10-K FY2025, Item 8; Q2 2026 call].

### Neutron

Neutron is a two-stage, 43-meter rocket designed to carry about 13,000 kg to low Earth orbit with a reusable first stage, powered by the company's own Archimedes engines and flying from Launch Complex 3 at Wallops Island, Virginia, licensed for two launches a year [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. It is meant to "enable significantly higher revenue per launch": management quotes "a $50 million to $55 million ASP" (average selling price) with "a commitment really not to do any significant discounting for early launches" [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Q2 2026 call].

Timeline. On January 21, 2026 the first-stage tank "ruptured" during a pressure test, and the February 10-K said "Neutron’s first launch is now targeted for Q4 2026" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A; Item 7]. By August the wording had softened: "Production of the Stage 1 tank is currently aligned with the target delivery of Neutron to the launch pad in Q4 2026. While the window for an end-of-year launch date is narrowing, we are balancing the schedule of the first launch with entering Neutron into service as a system ready for full-scale production" [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2].

Cost. **No filing breaks out Neutron's program cost**, capital spending or cumulative spend; R&D is one line and construction in progress is not attributed to programs [10-K FY2025, Note 2; Note 10]. What can be seen: total R&D rose from $41.8 million in FY2021 to $270.7 million in FY2025, and construction in progress from $27.3 million at end-2024 to $138.9 million at June 30, 2026 [10-K FY2023, Item 8; 10-K FY2025, Item 8; Note 10; 10-Q Q2 2026, Note 9]. Our inference, not a disclosed figure: most of that growth is Neutron, since the 10-K names it first among the reasons R&D rose and the CFO ties elevated capex to it [10-K FY2025, Item 7; Q2 2026 call]. Customers: no dollar figure for Neutron backlog is disclosed, but a slide (gatherer visual read) lists ten launches, one for Kepler Communications (no earlier than 2028), "7x confidential customer launches", the $397 million Space Force Flatellite mission "to launch on Neutron" and a U.S. Air Force experiment, plus National Security Space Launch provider status; the Q1 release counted "five new dedicated Neutron launches signed" in Q1 [Q2 2026 call; Q2 2026 release; Q2 2026 slides, p.26; p.27; Q1 2026 release].

## 5. Why customers don't leave

**A launch record and a schedule.** Electron has 87 successful missions against three failures (2020, 2021, 2023) and "in 2025 ... was the second most frequently launched orbital rocket" [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 1A; 10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. Management says the record is why customers are "locking in Neutron slots early" [Q2 2026 call]. Weakening sign: a failure and stand-down (each past failure stopped launches until the cause was found and the FAA, the U.S. launch-licensing regulator, authorized a return), or launch backlog shrinking [10-K FY2025, Item 1A].

**Making the parts it flies.** The company makes "many components and subsystems for our launch vehicles and family of spacecraft" and sells the same parts to other satellite programs [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. The CEO: "if you just turn up in the industry and say, I want 1,000 reaction wheels, then your chances of getting them are almost 0"; the CFO says owning the parts lets Rocket Lab "eliminate margin stacking" on satellite bids [Q2 2026 call]. Weakening sign: Space Systems gross margin sliding without recovering, or a rival component maker scaling up.

**Long programs with penalties for leaving.** Government satellite programs run "call it, 4 years", and contracts "typically include termination rights that may be exercised by customers upon advanced notice and payment of a specified termination fee" [Q2 2026 call; 10-K FY2025, Item 7]. It cuts both ways: on the program for MDA Corporation, a satellite-maker customer, MDA "is potentially entitled to claim liquidated damages" (a pre-agreed penalty) for late delivery [10-K FY2025, Note 3]. Weakening sign: "contract termination and study revenue" growing, and downward revenue adjustments on individual contracts [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2; Note 3].

**Government relationships and clearances.** Classified work requires facility and personnel security clearances and a board Government Security Committee [10-K FY2025, Item 1A; DEF 14A 2026, Proposal One]. Weakening sign: a large program awarded to a rival.

Caveat: most of this is scarcity, not lock-in; government contracts can be terminated "at their convenience", and SpaceX, Northrop Grumman, United Launch Alliance, Firefly and Blue Origin compete for launches [10-K FY2025, Item 1].

## 6. What could break it

Ranked from most to least plausible.

**1. Neutron fails or slips badly.** "Our future results will depend on the success of the development and commercial acceptance of our Neutron medium-capacity launch vehicle", and even after a first launch the company "could be unsuccessful in developing the ability to produce these launch vehicles in quantities" [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. SpaceX is on the competitor list, and "many of our competitors are larger and have substantially greater resources than we have"; the CEO's counter is that rivals' new capacity "is already spoken for, for their own internal programs" [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 1A; Q2 2026 call]. Early warning: the pad-delivery language in each release; a first-launch failure.

**2. One government customer is most of the growth.** An unnamed "Government customer" was 11% of revenue in FY2024, 28% in FY2025 and 42% in the first half of 2026 [10-K FY2025, Note 21; 10-Q Q2 2026, Note 3]. The top five customers were 49% of FY2025 revenue, and the top five backlog customers 77% of year-end backlog [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Government contracts "may be terminated or suspended by the government at any time", are fixed-price so "we bear the risk of loss if costs increase", and the October 2025 shutdown delayed awards, payments and export licences [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Early warning: the largest-customer percentage in each 10-Q.

**3. A fixed-price build goes wrong.** The 2022 MDA contract for 17 satellite buses is late, the customer may claim liquidated damages, and "it is not possible to determine with certainty the net amount, if any" [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 3]. Early warning: revenue revisions on individual contracts in Note 3.

**4. An Electron failure.** "Our launch services business is currently dependent on Electron", each past failure stopped launches until the FAA authorized a return, and insurance "will not protect us against our own losses" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Launch was 33% of FY2025 revenue and is 40% of backlog [10-K FY2025, Note 20; 10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2].

**5. Capital markets close.** The company funds its cash burn with share sales, and capital "might not be available on company favorable terms, if at all, or may be available only by diluting existing stockholders" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. The $2.4 billion on hand covers more than five years at the Q2 2026 burn rate (computed), but not the Iridium cash [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 5]. Early warning: ATM proceeds and the share count each quarter.

**6. The Iridium deal.** It needs "over $3.0 billion in cash" plus up to "approximately $1.8 billion" to refinance Iridium's loans, backed for now by a $3.6 billion 364-day bridge loan (a short-term loan to be replaced by permanent debt or equity) [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2; Note 1]. Closing "is not conditioned on our obtaining any financing", and the new debt could reduce funds available for Neutron [10-Q Q2 2026, Part II Item 1A]. If Iridium walks away in specified cases it owes a $223.62 million fee; no such fee is described for Rocket Lab [8-K 2026-06-29]. Combined debt after the deal, shares to be issued and cost-saving targets are not disclosed [10-Q Q2 2026, Part II Item 1A; Iridium deck 2026-06-29, p.11]. Early warning: the S-4 (the merger registration document), the Iridium shareholder vote, the permanent financing terms.

**7. Export rules and New Zealand.** Electron is built and mainly launched in New Zealand under U.S. export law (ITAR); licence delays hit foreign-customer launches in late 2025, and 15% of FY2025 spending was in foreign currencies, unhedged (not insured against currency swings) [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 1A; Item 7A].

**8. Key person.** Peter Beck "is the source of many, if not most, of the ideas and execution driving our company" and is not covered by key-person insurance [10-K FY2025, Item 1A].

## 7. Who runs it and what they do with the cash

Sir Peter Beck, 48, founded the company, has been CEO since July 2013 and chairman since August 2021, and is the only non-independent director on a seven-seat board [DEF 14A 2026, Proposal One; Corporate Governance]. His family trust holds 40.95 million Series A preferred shares that convert one-for-one, vote as if converted (so no extra voting power) and elect one director; they convert automatically if he leaves the CEO job (or another board-approved executive role), dies or is disabled, or if his stake through them falls below 5% [10-K FY2025, Note 13; 10-Q Q2 2026, Note 12]. He controls 7.5% of the votes [DEF 14A 2026, Security Ownership]. On March 30, 2026 he cut his salary from $800,000 to "$1.00 or the statutory minimum required under New Zealand law", waived any bonus and cancelled all 392,155 unvested restricted stock units (RSUs); the cancellation produced a one-time $11.2 million accounting charge [8-K 2026-03-30; 10-Q Q2 2026, Note 13; DEF 14A 2026, Summary Compensation Table]. His 2025 pay had been $6.8 million, mostly RSUs [DEF 14A 2026, Summary Compensation Table].

Adam Spice is CFO; his start date is not in the sources, and in 2025 he exercised options worth $86.1 million [DEF 14A 2026, Executive Compensation]. Pay is base salary plus time-based RSUs and a discretionary cash bonus, with no performance-vesting equity; a bonus plan with committee-set targets was adopted in August 2025, but for 2025 there were "No base salary increases" and "No discretionary cash bonuses" [DEF 14A 2026, CD&A]. Other holders above 5%: Khosla Ventures entities 6.1% and BlackRock 5.2% [DEF 14A 2026, Security Ownership].

## Glossary

- **Backlog** — signed contracts, excluding unexercised options, not yet delivered and not yet counted as revenue.
- **Adjusted EBITDA** — the company's own profit measure: earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization, further excluding stock-based pay, deal costs, currency gains or losses and similar items.
- **Non-GAAP free cash flow** — operating cash flow minus purchases of property, equipment and software; the company's measure of cash burn.
- **At-the-market (ATM) program** — an agreement under which banks sell the company's new shares gradually on the stock exchange at market prices.
- **Over-time revenue recognition** — booking revenue on a long build in proportion to costs incurred against the estimated total, rather than at delivery.
- **HASTE** — Hypersonic Accelerator Suborbital Test Electron; an Electron variant that flies test payloads on suborbital arcs (up and back down without completing an orbit) for "hypersonic and suborbital system technology development", including missile-defence testing (hypersonic: more than five times the speed of sound).
- **Liquidated damages** — a penalty written into a contract that the customer may claim if delivery is late.
- **ITAR** — U.S. export rules for defence-related technology that govern where rockets and satellites may be built, shipped and launched.
- **Series A preferred** — the founder's share class: converts one-for-one into common, votes as if converted, and elects one director.
- **GAAP / non-GAAP** — GAAP is the official accounting standard; "non-GAAP" is the company's adjusted version that removes stock-based pay, acquisition amortization and deal costs.
- **Capex** — capital expenditure; cash spent on equipment, buildings, launch sites and other long-lived assets, as opposed to day-to-day costs.
