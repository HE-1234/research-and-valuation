# Bloom Energy Corporation — The Business
_As of Q2 2026. Written 2026-09-09._

## 1. What they do

Bloom Energy makes and services the **Energy Server**, a box that turns natural gas (or biogas, hydrogen, or a blend) into electricity on the customer's own site. Inside is a solid-oxide fuel cell: stacks of ceramic plates at high temperature where fuel and air react chemically and release electrons, so nothing burns: "without combustion or moving parts", in the company's words [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. No flame means almost none of the smog-forming pollutants of an engine or turbine, and no water use in steady operation [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Sites range from "hundreds of kilowatts to hundreds of megawatts"; cells are printed in Fremont, California and assembled in Delaware [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. A second product, the Bloom Electrolyzer, makes hydrogen with the same cells; it is tiny so far [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Note 6].

Why does the site matter? Because the customer does not wait for the grid. A grid connection for a new data center can take years; a Bloom "islanded" microgrid, one not connected to the grid at all, lets a builder "skip the interconnection queue and start construction" [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. Bloom says "onsite generation can enable power availability within approximately 90 days" in some projects [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Who pays: data-center operators (hyperscalers, neoclouds and colocation landlords, who rent data-center space to tenants; see Glossary), commercial and industrial companies, and utilities [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. The U.S. was 90% of Q2 2026 revenue; South Korea is the second market, with "nearly 682 MW" installed through the SK group as distributor [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 1; 10-K FY2025, Item 1].

History. KR Sridhar founded the company in 2001; "It took Dr. Sridhar and his founding team nine years to get its solid oxide fuel cell to commercial production" [8-K 2026-06-17, Item 5.02; DEF 14A 2026]. It listed on the NYSE in July 2018, entered Korea that year through SK ecoplant, and opened the Fremont factory in July 2022 [10-K FY2025, Note 10; Item 7; Item 2]. It lost money for most of its life. The turn came in 2025: an up-to-$5 billion project-financing partnership with Brookfield, a partnership and stock warrant with Oracle, a $2.5 billion zero-interest convertible bond, and a product backlog that "more than doubled" [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Note 3; Note 8; DEF 14A 2026]. In Q2 2026 quarterly revenue passed $1 billion for the first time, up 165.5% on the year, led by "a significant deployment for a large AI infrastructure customer" the filings do not name [Q2 2026 release; 10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2].

## 2. How the money comes in

Bloom reports one segment and four revenue lines [10-K FY2025, Note 14]. **Product** is the sale of Energy Servers, recognized on delivery, so it is lumpy [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Note 3]. **Installation** is site work; from 2026 Bloom steers large customers to certified third-party installers, "resulting in lower installation revenue for Bloom" [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. **Service** is the maintenance contract sold with every unit: terms "range from 5 to 20 years, subject to termination for convenience on an annual basis", and the CEO says there is "a 100% attach rate between our product sales and our service" [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Q1 2026 call]. **Electricity** is the legacy model in which Bloom kept the server and sold power under a power purchase agreement (PPA); those entities were sold in 2022–2023, and the line now comes from servers Bloom still owns under its older Managed Services leases [10-K FY2025, Note 11; Note 2; Item 7].

Who the buyer is matters. Most end customers "prefer to pay for the power they will consume", so the sale "is made to a financier or other strategic partner that owns the Energy Server" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Since August 2025 the main financier is Brookfield: a "prospective financing framework of up to $5.0 billion over five years", each project held in a joint venture (a "Fund JV") in which Bloom keeps a passive stake of at most 9.9% (one project outside the fund carries 15%) and Brookfield gets "exclusivity over certain types of Bloom fuel cell projects" [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Note 7]. The Fund JV, not the data center, is Bloom's contractual customer; because Bloom owns a slice, the sales count as related-party revenue, and Bloom's share of the profit is deferred and returns over the equipment's life through the "equity in earnings (loss) of unconsolidated affiliates" line [10-K FY2025, Note 1; Note 7]. In Korea, SK ecoplant and SK eternix buy as distributors and resell (§7) [10-K FY2025, Item 7].

## 3. The economics

This is a factory business: cost per unit falls with volume and with each cheaper design. Product gross margin (what is left of product revenue after making the units) rose from 29% in FY2021 to 35–37% from FY2023 on, credited to "our continued efforts to reduce material, labor, and overhead costs through enhanced manufacturing processes and increased automation" [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. Q2 2026's 36.5% carried two one-offs: a $37.4 million refund of import tariffs a court ruled unlawful, worth about 4 points of margin, and a specific warranty reserve that grew from $19.7 million at March 31 to $58.3 million at June 30, so roughly $38.6 million (computed) was charged against Q2 product cost [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2; Note 6; 10-Q Q1 2026, Note 6].

**Why service lost money for years.** A service contract promises guaranteed output for up to 20 years at a fixed price, and fuel-cell stacks degrade and must be replaced [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. The line lost money every year from FY2021 to FY2024; FY2023 was the worst because "deployments of field replacement units" added $43.6 million of cost [10-K FY2023, Item 7]. FY2025 turned positive on "a reduction in the deployment of field replacement units, contributing to cost savings of $29.4 million" and more maintenance revenue, offset by "higher repair and overhaul expenses of $11.1 million, due to the aging fleet" [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. The CEO: "we have a roadmap to get to gross margin neutral and then gross margin positive and keep accreting", helped by "a digital twin associated to every single fuel cell stack" (a live computer model of each stack) [Q4 2025 call].

**Below gross profit, the costs are people.** FY2025 operating expenses were $514.6 million: research and development $186.0 million, sales and marketing $130.2 million (up 91.5%) and general and administrative $198.4 million [10-K FY2025, Item 8; Item 7]. These costs grow more slowly than revenue: management's chart has non-GAAP operating expenses falling from 26% of revenue in 2021 to a guided 13% in 2026 [Q2 2026 slides, p.7].

**Capital intensity is low for an energy company.** Fremont is being expanded "from 1 gigawatt to 2 gigawatts" of annual run-rate (yearly output pace) by the end of 2026, "can accommodate additional capacity expansion of up to approximately 5 gigawatts", and each further gigawatt should "require approximately six to nine months to install and capital expenditure of approximately $100 million to $150 million" [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. The CEO: "Our return on invested capital for capacity expansion is a few months, not years" [Q4 2025 call].

**Tax credits sit in the customer's economics.** Buyers claim a federal investment tax credit (ITC); the old Section 48 credit "expired at the end of 2024", and the July 2025 tax law "modified the 30% section 48E ITC to specifically include fuel cells" for construction starting in 2026–2033 [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Note 1]. Bloom relies on "tax equity financing arrangements to realize the benefits" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A].

For fifteen years it was not: the accumulated deficit (all past losses added up) reached $3.99 billion at the end of 2025 [10-K FY2025, Item 8]. Operating cash flow turned positive in 2024 [10-K FY2025, Item 8].

Besides SK ecoplant's preferred stock (a special share class, later converted into ordinary shares; §7), convertible notes raised $230.0 million (2020), $632.5 million (2023) and $402.5 million (2024) [10-K FY2025, Note 8]. In November 2025 Bloom sold $2.5 billion of notes that "will not bear regular interest", convertible at $194.97 a share and due November 2030, and used $988.4 million of the proceeds plus 42.4 million new shares to retire $975.9 million of 3% notes [8-K 2025-11-04, Item 1.01; Exhibit 99.2; 10-K FY2025, Note 8]. Return on capital, plainly: equity was $1.64 billion at June 30, 2026 against Q2 operating income of $182.2 million, so if Q2 repeats the business earns back a large share of its book equity (assets minus liabilities on the balance sheet) each year [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 1]. Profit is flattered by paying almost no income tax, because $2.3 billion of past federal losses shelter it [10-K FY2025, Note 15; 10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2].

## 5. Why customers don't leave

**Only Bloom makes Bloom's cells.** Bloom holds 380 active U.S. patents and "we are the only party who can manufacture our products", a fact the 10-K lists among the reasons financiers once hesitated [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 1A]. Weakening sign: product gross margin sliding while volume rises, or a rival non-combustion on-site generator winning a hyperscaler.

**The service annuity.** An annuity is a stream of payments running for years. Every sale carries a 5–20 year service contract, "on average, it's 10 to 15 years" in data centers, and service backlog was about $14.0 billion in February 2026 [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Q1 2026 call; Q4 2025 slides, p.6]. The caveat sits in the same 10-K sentence: "subject to termination for convenience on an annual basis" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Weakening sign: service gross margin falling back toward zero, or performance-guarantee payments rising ($13.8 million in the first half of 2026) [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2].

**Speed, while the alternatives are slow.** "Bloom will not be the bottleneck to your growth", backed by "delivering a hyperscale AI factory order in fifty-five days against a ninety-day commitment", while "major turbine OEMs are currently supply constrained" (OEMs: manufacturers) [Q4 2025 call; 10-K FY2025, Item 1]. This is scarcity, not lock-in. Weakening sign: turbine backlogs shortening, or grid queues clearing [10-K FY2025, Item 7].

**Approved-vendor status and repeat buying.** "Today, all the major US hyperscalers and over a dozen US neoclouds, AI labs, and colocation data center operators have validated and approved our power solutions", and master services agreements let a customer move a deployment "from 1 site to another" [Q2 2026 release; Q1 2026 call]. Weakening sign: a new largest customer every quarter with no repeat orders from earlier ones.

## 6. What could break it

Ranked from most to least plausible.

**1. The AI data-center boom slows or is blocked.** "Slower expansion of AI data centers due to actual or perceived deceleration in AI adoption or other factors could have an adverse impact" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Opposition is rising: "in July 2026, the governor of New York state signed an executive order to create a moratorium on new hyperscale data centers" (a temporary ban) [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. Early warning: customer deposits and the largest-customer share each quarter.

**2. A handful of buyers.** In Q2 2026 "revenue from one customer*, which is not our related party, accounted for approximately 73% of our total revenue"; for the six months two customers were 44% and 21%, the second being the Brookfield Fund JVs (the 10-Q/A corrected a three-month / six-month transposition in the original 10-Q) [10-Q/A Q2 2026, Note 1]. In FY2025 three customers were 43% (the Fund JVs), 13% and 12% [10-K FY2025, Note 1]. Three customers held 36%, 34% and 17% of receivables (money owed to Bloom by customers) at June 30, 2026 [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 1]. Early warning: the Note 1 percentages and related-party revenue.

**3. The fleet underperforms.** Performance-guarantee payments are "capped at an aggregate total of approximately $846.1 million" with "approximately $468.9 million" of remaining potential payment at June 30, 2026 [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. Accrued warranty (money set aside for expected repairs) jumped from $20.0 million to $77.8 million in six months, including the $58.3 million specific reserve [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 6]. Early warning: the accrued-warranty line and service gross margin.

**4. Tax credits and project finance.** Backlog "reflects anticipated ITC", and deployment "is directly tied to our ability to secure project financing" [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 1A]. Early warning: Fund JV revenue and the "Equity in earnings (loss) of unconsolidated affiliates" line.

**5. Gas, competitors and the grid.** The economics "depend on both the price of gas available from the local gas utilities and the cost of electricity available from alternative sources" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]; competitors include gas turbines, solar plus storage and small nuclear reactors [10-K FY2025, Item 1].

**6. Supply chain.** Some inputs come from "sole suppliers" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. After a short seller's report on July 8, 2026, Bloom said it "categorically rejects the Report's claims regarding the Company's financial results and accounting" and, on the scandium oxide the cells use, "We have sufficient supply of scandium oxide to meet our current fuel cell demand and backlog, and our supply is not dependent on China" [8-K 2026-07-09, Item 7.01].

**7. The $2.5 billion bill in 2030.** The 0% notes convert only above $194.97; if the stock is below that in November 2030 they must be repaid in cash [10-K FY2025, Note 8].

**8. Korea.** The second market rests on the SK group, whose distribution agreement runs to December 31, 2027, and on a clean-hydrogen mandate that replaced the old renewables standard [10-K FY2025, Item 7; Item 1A].

**9. Key person.** The board's June 2026 grant cites "the need to retain Dr. Sridhar" (§7) [8-K 2026-06-17, Item 5.02].

## 7. Who runs it and what they do with the cash

KR Sridhar, 65, is founder, chairman and CEO; he owns 2.0% and also votes SK ecoplant's remaining 2.5% under "an irrevocable proxy to, Dr. Sridhar at his discretion" [DEF 14A 2026]. The CFO seat was covered by the chief accounting officer from May 2025 until Simon Edwards started April 13, 2026 [DEF 14A 2026; 8-K 2026-03-26, Item 5.02]. Director Michael Boskin also sits on Oracle's board [424B7 2026-04-27]. Large holders: Ameriprise 10.7%, BlackRock 7.9%, Vanguard 6.9% [DEF 14A 2026].

**SK ecoplant's dual role.** It is distributor, customer and former shareholder. Its distribution agreement carries "purchase commitments ... on a take-or-pay basis" of 500 megawatts for 2024–2027, and if it falls short "we would be entitled to damages equivalent to the lost profit"; progress against the commitment is not disclosed [10-K FY2025, Note 17]. It bought $255.0 million of Series A preferred in 2021 and $311.0 million of Series B in 2023, both converted into common stock; from September 23, 2023 it was a related party holding 23,491,701 shares, 10.3% at the end of 2024 [10-K FY2025, Note 17; Note 12]. On July 10, 2025 it sold 10 million shares, fell to 5.8% and "ceased to be a related party", then sold down to 2.5% by year-end; the proxy given to the CEO covers those 6,971,701 shares [10-K FY2025, Note 12; DEF 14A 2026].

**Pay.** The CEO's December 2024 package (1,500,000 performance units, which are shares earned by hitting targets, 500,000 time units, earned by staying, and a 600,000-unit "Replacement Grant") put his reported 2024 pay at $45.0 million; the FY2025 tranche (portion) paid out at the 300% maximum [DEF 14A 2026]. Shareholders gave "approximately 62% support" in 2025, objecting to "the multi-year nature of the CEO equity grant and the absence of disclosed numerical targets"; the 2026 vote passed with 96.5% of votes for or against (180,455,738 to 6,518,825; 96.3% counting abstentions) (computed) [DEF 14A 2026; 8-K 2026-05-27, Item 5.07]. On June 15, 2026 the board added 319,082 target performance units, payable up to 300% on the best four consecutive quarters of revenue through 2029, citing "stockholder feedback emphasizing the need to retain Dr. Sridhar as Chief Executive Officer beyond 2027, when his previous equity award from 2024 will vest" [8-K 2026-06-17, Item 5.02; 8-K/A 2026-06-22].

## Glossary

- **Solid-oxide fuel cell** — a ceramic cell that, at high temperature, combines fuel and oxygen electrochemically to produce electricity directly, without combustion or moving parts.
- **Hyperscaler / neocloud** — the largest cloud-computing companies (hyperscalers) and newer companies renting out AI computing capacity (neoclouds); both build the data centers Bloom powers.
- **Islanded microgrid** — an on-site power system not connected to the electric grid at all, so it needs no grid-connection approval.
- **ITC (investment tax credit)** — a U.S. federal tax credit worth a percentage of a project's cost, claimed by whoever owns the equipment; Section 48 was the old rule, Section 48E the new one.
- **Tax equity** — investors who fund a project chiefly to use its tax credits; most U.S. Bloom projects need one.
- **PPA (power purchase agreement)** — a contract to buy electricity at a set price per kilowatt-hour from equipment someone else owns.
- **Take-or-pay** — a purchase commitment the buyer must pay for whether or not it takes delivery.
- **Convertible notes** — bonds the holder may swap for shares at a set price; the 0% notes convert at $194.97.
- **Unsatisfied (remaining) performance obligations** — signed contract value not yet recognized as revenue, excluding contracts of a year or less; narrower than backlog.
- **Non-GAAP** — the company's adjusted figures, which mainly add back stock-based pay; GAAP is the official U.S. accounting standard.
- **Capex** — capital expenditure: cash spent on factories, equipment and buildings rather than day-to-day costs.
- **Adjusted EBITDA** — the company's profit measure before interest, tax, depreciation, stock-based pay and a few other items.
