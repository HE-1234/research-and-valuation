# Broadcom Inc. — The Business
_As of Q2 FY2026 (quarter ended May 3, 2026). Written 2026-09-07._

## 1. What they do

Broadcom is two businesses under one roof. The first designs chips and has almost all of them made by outside factories: in the first half of FY2026 about 95% of the wafers produced by its contract manufacturers came from one company, TSMC in Taiwan, while Broadcom keeps a few small plants for specialised parts such as radio filters and lasers [10-Q Q2 FY2026, Item 1A; 10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Its chips go into "enterprise and artificial intelligence ("AI") data centers, servers and networking and connectivity equipment", plus storage, broadband, telecom, smartphones and factories [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. The buyers are cloud giants (hyperscalers), AI model developers and makers of servers, switches, set-top boxes and phones, mostly via distributors [10-K FY2025, Item 1].

The second business is infrastructure software, mostly VMware, bought in November 2023 [10-K FY2025, Note 4]. VMware's main product, VMware Cloud Foundation (VCF), lets a big company run its own data center the way a cloud provider runs one: "a private cloud platform that integrates compute, storage, networking, and management into a single solution" [10-K FY2025, Note 4]. Around it sit mainframe software, Symantec security and fibre-channel storage networking, sold to "large enterprises", including "most of the Fortune 500", which pay recurring fees for software their operations already run on [10-K FY2025, Item 1].

The modern company was Avago Technologies, listed in August 2009, which bought LSI in 2014 and Broadcom Corporation in February 2016, taking its name [DEF 14A 2026, CD&A; Corporate Governance; 10-K FY2025, Item 1]. CA, Inc. and Symantec Enterprise Security followed (named in the filings, undated), then VMware on November 22, 2023 for $86.3 billion in cash and Broadcom stock [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Note 4].

**AI chips versus the rest.** Management splits chip revenue into "AI semiconductor revenue" and everything else. The AI part has two pieces: custom AI accelerators, which Broadcom calls XPUs, processors designed to one customer's specification for training and running AI models, with Google's TPU the best-known example; and AI networking, the Ethernet switch chips (Tomahawk, Jericho), network cards and optical parts that connect thousands of accelerators into one machine [10-K FY2025, Item 1; 8-K 2026-04-06; Q2 FY2026 call]. "The rest" is the older lines: broadband, wireless parts for phones, storage controllers, office networking and industrial [10-K FY2025, Item 1].

Chips are sold per unit on purchase orders, and revenue is booked when they reach the distributor, "which can cause our quarterly net revenue to fluctuate significantly" [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. Distributors took 48% of revenue in FY2025 and 56% in the first half of FY2026; "Direct sales to one semiconductor solutions customer, which is a distributor, accounted for 42% of our net revenue" in Q2 FY2026, against 32% for FY2025 [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 7; 10-Q Q2 FY2026, Item 1A; Item 2]. Behind the distributors, "aggregate sales to our top five end customers, through all channels, accounted for approximately 45% of our net revenue" in Q2 FY2026, up from "approximately 40%" in FY2025 [10-Q Q2 FY2026, Item 2; 10-K FY2025, Item 1]. The FY2023 10-K put Apple at "approximately 20%" of revenue; the FY2025 10-K does not mention Apple [10-K FY2023, Item 7; 10-K FY2025].

AI semiconductor revenue is a management figure, not an audited line: the 10-K and 10-Q never state it; the proxy statement (the document sent to shareholders before the annual meeting) and each quarterly release do (table) [10-K FY2025; 10-Q Q2 FY2026; DEF 14A 2026, Proxy Statement Summary; Q2 FY2026 release]. The split between accelerators and networking is not disclosed in any filing; the CEO said "Networking represented almost 40% of our Q2 AI revenue" [Q2 FY2026 call].

Software is sold on multi-year contracts. Where the customer can cancel at will, revenue is spread evenly over the term; where it cannot, the licence part is booked upfront, and $7.8 billion of FY2025 revenue was such upfront licences [10-K FY2025, Note 2; Note 3]. VMware used to sell perpetual licences (pay once, own forever); Broadcom moved it to VCF subscriptions, so quarterly software revenue now swings with how many contracts signed lack a cancellation right [10-K FY2025, Item 7; 10-Q Q2 FY2026, Item 2]. The jump from $7.6 billion in FY2023 to $21.5 billion in FY2024 is the purchase of VMware, which brought $12.4 billion of FY2024 revenue, not organic growth; the 26% rise in FY2025 came from "strong demand for our VCF product ... and the transition to a subscription license model" [10-K FY2025, Note 4; Item 7]. VMware's own revenue and renewal rates are not disclosed; the call gave only "ARR growth of 17% year-over-year" [Q2 FY2026 call].

## 3. The economics

Every chip needs a wafer from TSMC plus packaging and testing, so cost rises with each unit: cost of products sold was $12.1 billion on $44.8 billion of products revenue in FY2025, and TSMC "has raised, and may in the future raise, their prices" [10-K FY2025, Item 8; Item 1A]. Software costs almost nothing extra per customer: $1.9 billion of cost on $27.0 billion of segment revenue, a gross margin (revenue left after the direct cost of making the product, as a share of revenue) the CFO put at "93%" in Q2 FY2026 against "approximately 70%" for chips [10-K FY2025, Note 13; Q2 FY2026 call]. The big fixed cost is research and development, $11.0 billion in FY2025, which does not rise with volume [10-K FY2025, Item 8].

Software also earns more because Broadcom cut VMware's costs: FY2025 software margin rose on "lower infrastructure software labor costs following our integration of the VMware business" [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. The mix is now moving toward chips, and within chips toward the thinnest-margin ones: "our ASICs, TPUs, some of the wireless business has lower margins" [Q2 FY2026 call]. That is why non-GAAP gross margin fell from 79.4% to 77.1% in a year while operating margin rose (computed): custom accelerators carry a lower gross margin but almost no extra operating cost [Q2 FY2026 release].

**Capital intensity.** Tiny in the usual sense: capex has run 1.0% to 1.6% of revenue, below depreciation until FY2025, because the factories belong to TSMC and software needs none [10-K FY2023, Item 8; 10-K FY2025, Item 8]. The new claim on capital is different. At May 3, 2026 Broadcom had $128,110 million of "unconditional purchase commitments to purchase goods or services, primarily inventory", $55,214 million due in FY2027 and $72,870 million in FY2028; six months earlier the same table totalled $132 million [10-Q Q2 FY2026, Note 10; 10-K FY2025, Note 14]. Inventory nearly doubled in the same six months, to $4,328 million, "primarily to support higher expected shipments for custom AI accelerators" [10-Q Q2 FY2026, Item 2]. Broadcom has promised suppliers roughly two years of AI-chip purchases, matched by $164.6 billion of committed customer contracts (§5), but the supply promise binds whatever demand does (our inference) [10-Q Q2 FY2026, Note 2].

Free cash flow was close to half of revenue in FY2021–FY2023 and 42% in FY2025 after VMware's interest and restructuring bills [10-K FY2023, Item 8; 10-K FY2025, Item 8]. Cash flow runs well above net income because amortization and stock pay are added back; net income also swings on tax (a 37.8% rate in FY2024 from a one-off transfer of intellectual property (IP) rights to the United States, a net benefit in FY2025) [10-K FY2025, Item 7; Note 12]. Two things flatter the cash line: Broadcom sells customer receivables (unpaid invoices) to banks for early cash, $7.4 billion in FY2025, and in FY2026 it stopped buying shares to cover employees' tax on vesting, a $3.9 billion outflow in FY2025 [10-K FY2025, Note 5; 10-Q Q2 FY2026, Item 2].

**Debt and taxes.** Principal (the amount borrowed, before interest) was $66,720 million at May 3, 2026, all fixed-rate, costing $3,210 million of interest in FY2025, about 13% of operating income (computed) [10-Q Q2 FY2026, Note 6; 10-K FY2025, Item 8]. In January 2026 Broadcom sold $4.5 billion of notes (bonds) due 2031 to 2056 at 4.300% to 5.700% and used the money to redeem notes due 2027 and 2028 [8-K 2026-01-13]. Only $493 million comes due in FY2027, and a $7.5 billion bank credit line is unused [10-Q Q2 FY2026, Note 6]. Against $26.9 billion of yearly free cash flow the debt is heavy in dollars but light in practice (our inference). Taxes are rising: Singapore and Malaysian tax incentives cut FY2025 tax by about $2,709 million, but the global minimum tax applies from FY2026 and management expects a non-GAAP tax rate of "approximately 16%" [10-K FY2025, Item 7; Q2 FY2026 call].

**Return on capital**, honestly. Of $171.1 billion of assets at FY2025 year-end, $97.8 billion was goodwill and $32.3 billion acquired intangibles, the price of past deals [10-K FY2025, Item 8]. Free cash flow of $26.9 billion is about 16% of that total (computed): a modest return on everything ever paid for the businesses, far higher on the tangible assets alone (our inference). Whether VMware earns its $86.3 billion depends on customers renewing their VCF subscriptions; no VMware-only profit is disclosed [10-K FY2025, Note 4].

## 5. Why customers don't leave

**Custom chips: years of co-design, now backed by contracts.** An XPU is built "to our individual customers' specifications", and "Many of our major customer relationships have been in place for many years and are often the result of years of collaborative product development" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Once a hyperscaler builds data centers around a chip, switching means redesigning the chip and its software, a multi-year job (our inference). The contracts now match: in April 2026 Google signed "a Long Term Agreement for Broadcom to develop and supply custom Tensor Processing Units ("TPUs") for Google's future generations of TPUs and a Supply Assurance Agreement ... through up to 2031" [8-K 2026-04-06]. Firmly committed contracts (RPO) jumped from $33.3 billion at FY2025 year-end to $164.6 billion at May 3, 2026, and these commitments "include obligations under a long-term contract for custom AI accelerators" [10-K FY2025, Note 3; 10-Q Q2 FY2026, Note 2]. Sign of weakening: the CEO already concedes "we fully expect that there will be some diversity of sources" at Google; watch for a second supplier named, RPO falling, or the top-five share dropping [Q2 FY2026 call; 10-Q Q2 FY2026, Item 2].

**Networking: first to each speed step.** Management claims "at least one generation of technology and product leadership" and "the industry's only 100 terabit Ethernet switch, the Tomahawk 6, for over one year" [Q2 FY2026 call]. This is a head start renewed each generation, not a lock, and Broadcom's own IP licensing has "enabled some of our competitors" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Sign of weakening: a rival shipping a same-speed switch first, or networking's share of AI revenue falling faster than the "closer to around 30%" management expects [Q2 FY2026 call].

**Software: the cost of moving a data center.** VMware sits beneath the applications of "most of the Fortune 500"; replacing it means re-platforming everything above it, and contracts run for years [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 1A]. Broadcom used that position to move customers onto VCF subscriptions, which is why software grew 26% in FY2025 [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. Sign of weakening: growth below the 31% guided for Q3, or the regulatory "investigations or inquiries ... in Korea, Japan and the European Union into certain of our contracting and business practices" becoming cases [Q2 FY2026 call; 10-Q Q2 FY2026, Item 1A].

## 6. What could break it

Ranked from most to least plausible.

**1. A handful of customers who can design their own chips.** Five end customers are about 45% of revenue and one distributor 42%; none is named [10-Q Q2 FY2026, Item 2]. The AI buyers have their own chip teams, and Broadcom lists customers proceeding "with customer-owned tooling" as a route to lower demand [10-Q Q2 FY2026, Item 1A]. Early warning: the concentration percentages in each 10-Q; a customer adding a second accelerator supplier. Exposure: AI was about 49% of Q2 revenue (computed) [Q2 FY2026 release].

**2. The AI spending cycle turns while Broadcom holds the inventory.** Until FY2025 Broadcom carried almost no purchase commitments; now it carries $128.1 billion plus doubled inventory, and warns that some AI customers "may have constrained resources or capital and may be unable to pay for their required AI infrastructure" [10-Q Q2 FY2026, Note 10; 10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. A pause in cloud spending would leave it obliged to buy chips it cannot sell. Early warning: inventory and commitments rising faster than RPO; the $56 billion FY2026 or "in excess of $100 billion" FY2027 AI figures cut [Q2 FY2026 call]. Exposure: the AI profit pool and the balance sheet.

**3. Financing structures shift customer risk onto Broadcom.** On June 8, 2026 Broadcom arranged for Apollo to take on "agreements to purchase AI racks based on custom AI accelerators designed by us and the related lease agreements with a customer", and gave Apollo a backstop on "the customer's lease obligations over 5-year terms ... with a maximum exposure of $29 billion" [10-Q Q2 FY2026, Note 11; Part II Item 5]. In plain words: if that unnamed customer stops paying its lease, Broadcom pays Apollo instead, up to $29 billion, with the racks as fallback. The CEO also described an "AI XPU platform with Apollo and Blackstone and other leading investors to deploy more than 20 gigawatts of compute capacity through 2028", first slice (tranche) "valued at $35 billion"; Broadcom's exposure to it is not disclosed [Q2 FY2026 call]. Early warning: new backstops or guarantees in the 10-Q notes. Exposure: up to $29 billion, growing "as the AI racks are deployed" [10-Q Q2 FY2026, Note 11].

**4. TSMC and Taiwan.** About 95% of outsourced wafers come from TSMC, most products are "designed to be manufactured in a specific process, typically at one particular fab or foundry", and Broadcom does "not generally have long-term capacity commitments" with its manufacturers [10-Q Q2 FY2026, Item 1A; 10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. A disruption in Taiwan would stop most chip revenue; the filings offer no early warning.

**5. VMware customers push back.** Businesses may "shift existing compute workloads off-premises to public cloud providers" or "not accept our subscription licensing model" [10-Q Q2 FY2026, Item 1A]. Early warning: software growth below guidance. Exposure: $20.8 billion of FY2025 segment operating income [10-K FY2025, Note 13].

**6. Export controls and China.** Shipments to China including Hong Kong were 17% of FY2025 revenue, though "a substantially smaller percentage" depends on Chinese end customers; U.S. restrictions "have had and may in the future have an adverse effect on our revenue" [10-K FY2025, Item 7; Item 1A]. Early warning: new U.S. rules and changed risk-factor wording.

**7. Hock Tan.** He is 74, and "None of our senior management is bound by written employment contracts. In addition, we do not currently maintain key person life insurance" [10-K FY2025, Item 1; Item 1A]. The board has succession plans but names no one [DEF 14A 2026, Corporate Governance].

**8. Debt.** $66.7 billion is manageable on today's cash flow; the danger is scenario 2 arriving with the debt, the commitments and the backstop all at once [10-Q Q2 FY2026, Note 6; Item 1A].

## 7. Who runs it and what they do with the cash

Hock Tan has been President and CEO since March 2006 [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. His pay is two performance share awards and no annual cash bonus. The 2023 award pays up to 10,000,000 shares if the stock clears price hurdles and he stays through October 31, 2027 [DEF 14A 2026, CD&A]. The 2025 award, granted September 3, 2025 "to extend his leadership of Broadcom through fiscal 2030", pays 0% to 300% of 610,521 target shares on one metric, "AI Revenue" in the best four consecutive quarters of FY2028–FY2030: nothing at or below $60 billion, 100% at $90 billion, 200% at $105 billion, 300% at or above $120 billion [DEF 14A 2026, Letter from the Chairman; CD&A]. Reported FY2025 pay was $205.3 million, almost all that grant; the pay vote passed with 66% of votes cast (computed), down from 92% a year earlier [DEF 14A 2026, Executive Compensation; CD&A; 8-K 2026-04-21]. The incentive rewards revenue, not margin or cash (our view).

CFO Kirsten Spears retires June 12, 2026; Amie Thuener, "Vice President, Corporate Controller and Chief Accounting Officer of Alphabet Inc. since 2018", succeeds her [10-K FY2025, Item 1; 8-K 2026-04-02]. Alphabet is Google's parent (our observation; the 8-K does not comment). After the April 2026 meeting the board has eight directors, seven independent, chaired by Henry Samueli, co-founder of the original Broadcom Corporation, who owns 1.8% and has pledged 16,175,000 shares as loan collateral [DEF 14A 2026, Board of Directors; Security Ownership; CD&A; 8-K 2026-04-21]. Vanguard holds 9.9%, BlackRock 7.3%, all insiders 1.9%; Mr. Tan directly holds 908,474 shares before unvested awards [DEF 14A 2026, Security Ownership].

## Glossary

- **XPU** — Broadcom's name for a custom AI accelerator: a processor designed to one cloud customer's specification to train and run AI models.
- **TPU** — Tensor Processing Unit, Google's own AI accelerator, which Broadcom co-designs and supplies.
- **ASIC** — application-specific integrated circuit; a chip built for one customer or one job rather than sold off the shelf; XPUs are ASICs.
- **Ethernet switch** — the chip that directs data traffic between servers; in AI data centers it links thousands of accelerators into one system.
- **Private cloud** — a company's own data center run with software that lets one physical server act as many separate virtual computers, the way a cloud provider's does.
- **Perpetual licence vs subscription** — a perpetual licence is paid once and owned forever; a subscription is paid each year and stops when payment stops.
- **RPO** — remaining performance obligations; revenue under signed contracts that the customer cannot cancel and Broadcom has not yet delivered.
- **Foundry** — a factory that manufactures chips designed by others; TSMC is the world's largest.
- **Wafer** — the thin silicon disc on which hundreds of chips are made at once before being cut apart.
- **Tape-out** — the point when a chip design is finished and sent to the foundry to be manufactured.
- **ARR** — annual recurring revenue; the yearly value of subscription contracts currently in force, a figure Broadcom gives only as a growth rate.
- **GAAP / non-GAAP** — GAAP is the official accounting standard; non-GAAP is the company's version excluding acquisition amortization, stock-based pay, restructuring and similar items.
- **Adjusted EBITDA** — non-GAAP operating income with depreciation added back (plus other income); Broadcom guides to it as a percentage of revenue.
- **Amortization** — spreading the price paid for acquired technology and customer relationships over several years as a yearly expense, with no cash leaving in those years.
- **Goodwill** — the part of an acquisition price above the value of the assets actually bought; it sits on the balance sheet until the company decides it is worth less and writes it down.
- **Capex** — capital expenditure; cash spent on equipment and buildings, as opposed to day-to-day costs.
- **Gigawatt** — a unit of electrical power; management measures AI compute capacity by the power the data center draws, so "10 gigawatts" means enough chips to fill data centers using that much power.
- **Backstop** — a promise to cover someone else's payments if they default.
