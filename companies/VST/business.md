# Vistra Corp. — The Business
_As of Q2 2026. Written 2026-09-09._

## 1. What they do

Vistra owns about 44,000 megawatts of power plants and sells the electricity two ways: into wholesale markets, and directly to about 5 million homes and businesses through retail brands such as TXU Energy [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. It calls itself "an integrated retail electricity and power generation company" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. The plants run on natural gas (62% of capacity), coal (20%), nuclear (15%) and solar or batteries (3%), mostly in Texas and the PJM grid in the east [10-K FY2025, Item 1].

A plant owner here does not set its price. Every generator sells into a pool and "all generators receive the same price for energy based on the bid price of the last MWh needed to balance supply and demand" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Vistra's profit is the gap between that price and its own cost, so a cheap-fuel nuclear plant earns most when a costly gas plant sets the price, which is the normal case: "Wholesale electricity prices generally move with natural gas prices, except in certain circumstances, such as when ERCOT power prices increase significantly during extreme weather events due to generation scarcity" [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. Vistra sells most of its expected output years ahead to tame those swings (§2, §3).

History: the predecessor, Energy Future Holdings, filed for bankruptcy in April 2014, and Vistra emerged from that reorganization on October 3, 2016 [10-K FY2022, Glossary] [10-K FY2025, Glossary]. It merged with Dynegy in April 2018 [10-K FY2022, Glossary]. Winter Storm Uri in February 2021 cost it $2.2 billion of pre-tax earnings in one week (§6) [10-K FY2022, Item 7]. It bought Energy Harbor (4,048 MW of PJM nuclear plants and a retail book) in March 2024 and seven Lotus gas plants in October 2025, and a ~5,500 MW Cogentrix gas fleet is under contract to close "in late 2026" [10-K FY2025, Item 1] [10-K FY2025, Note 2] [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2].

In Q4 2024 the Sunset segment was removed and its plants moved into Texas and East "based on their respective geographies"; only FY2023 was recast [10-K FY2025, Note 21].

**Retail.** TXU Energy and five sister brands serve "approximately 5 million customers" in 16 states, 2.6 million of them in Texas [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Retail is also the shock absorber: "Our integrated power generation and retail electricity business provides flexibility to hedge our generation position by utilizing retail markets as an effective sales channel" [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. When wholesale prices spike, generation gains and retail, having promised customers a price, loses; the reverse when prices fall: the two are "to some extent countercyclical in nature" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A].

**Wholesale energy (Texas, East, West).** 2025 wholesale generation revenue from grid operators was $3.2 billion, other wholesale contracts $1.1 billion, realized hedge gains $583 million [10-K FY2025, Note 3]. Hedges are forward sales at a locked price: as of August 3, 2026 Vistra had sold "approximately 100% of its expected generation volumes for 2026, approximately 94% for 2027, and approximately 72% for 2028" [Q2 2026 release, p.2].

**Capacity payments (mainly PJM).** Outside Texas, grid operators also pay plants for being available: "PJM also administers a forward capacity auction, the Reliability Pricing Model (RPM), which establishes a long-term market for capacity" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Vistra cleared about 10,314 MW at $329.17 per MW-day for June 2026–May 2027, 10,566 MW at $333.44 for 2027–2028 and 10,924 MW at $325.00 for 2028–2029 [8-K 2025-07-23, Item 8.01] [8-K 2025-12-17, Item 8.01] [8-K 2026-07-14, Item 8.01]. Texas has no capacity market; "ERCOT relies primarily on energy-market price signals" [10-K FY2025, Item 1].

**Nuclear production tax credit (PTC).** The 2022 Inflation Reduction Act pays existing nuclear plants "up to $15 per MWh" for 2024–2032, phasing out as the plant's own revenue rises between "$26.00 per MWh and $44.75 per MWh" (the 2025 thresholds, adjusted for inflation each year) [10-K FY2025, Note 5]. It is a floor in weak years: PTC revenue was $545 million for 2024 and $220 million for 2025, when prices were higher [10-K FY2025, Note 5]. Guidance "excludes any potential benefit from the nuclear production tax credit" [Q2 2026 release, p.13].

**Long-term data-center contracts.** Twenty-year PPAs swap market risk for a fixed customer over a large share of a plant: Amazon Web Services takes 1,200 MW of Comanche Peak's 2,400 MW, deliveries "to begin in the fourth quarter of 2027 and ramp to full capacity by 2032"; Meta takes 2,609 MW at the PJM nuclear plants (including 433 MW of uprates, output added by upgrading the plants), starting "in late 2026", uprates 2031–2034 [10-K FY2025, Item 7] [8-K 2026-01-09, Item 7.01]. Prices are not disclosed; each deal is described only as roughly 8–10% accretion (increase) to Adjusted FCFbG, plus "an additional approximately 5%-7%" for Meta's uprates [8-K 2025-09-29, Item 8.01] [8-K 2026-01-09, Item 7.01]. Management says Retail plus these contracts will supply nearly 50% of EBITDA [Q2 2026 slides, p.9]. In June 2026 it committed "up to $1 billion" to Helix, a KKR-led data-center fund, as "preferred power partner"; no megawatts or prices are disclosed [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 7] [Q2 2026 call, l.31].

## 3. The economics

**Cost is fuel plus fixed plant cost.** Fuel and purchased power (the gas and coal the plants burn, and the power Retail buys) move with commodity prices; plant operating costs ($2.8 billion in 2025), depreciation ($2.0 billion, the yearly write-down of plant cost) and overhead ($1.7 billion) barely move, so a wider spread between power and fuel is almost all profit and a narrower one leaves the fixed costs standing [10-K FY2025, Item 8]. The spread has a name: "The wholesale market price of electricity divided by the market price of natural gas represents the Market Heat Rate"; nuclear and coal plants gain when gas or the heat rate rises, gas plants only from the heat rate [10-K FY2025, Item 7].

**Why GAAP margins jump: the hedges.** Hedges are revalued every quarter against forward prices (today's quotes for future delivery). "When power prices increase or decrease compared to what our generation segments have sold forward, the generation segments recognize unrealized losses or gains, respectively"; Retail, which has bought that power, "experiences an inverse effect" [10-K FY2025, Item 7]. The swings sit inside revenue: a $2.5 billion unrealized loss in 2022, a $1.2 billion gain in 2024, an $808 million loss in 2025 and a $472 million loss in Q2 2026 [10-K FY2023, Item 8] [10-K FY2025, Item 8] [Q2 2026 release, p.1]. None is cash; the cash arrives when the hedged power is delivered at the locked price.

**The two measures management steers by.** The CEO "primarily focuses on Adjusted EBITDA" [10-K FY2025, Note 21]: net income before interest, taxes and depreciation, with "unrealized gains or losses from hedging activities", merger costs, stock pay and "certain other items" removed [Q2 2026 release, p.5]. Adjusted Free Cash Flow before Growth (Adjusted FCFbG) is "cash from operating activities excluding changes in margin deposits and working capital and adjusted for maintenance capital expenditures, other net investment activities, and other items": operating cash flow, ignoring hedge collateral, minus upkeep capex, before growth capex [Q2 2026 release, p.5]. "Ongoing Operations" means excluding Asset Closure [Q2 2026 release, p.5]. Both are management's constructions; the cash-flow statement in §4 is the fact base, and the bonus plan pays on both (§7).

**Capital intensity.** Upkeep runs about $1.0 billion a year plus $300–475 million of nuclear fuel; the 2026 plan totals about $3.0 billion, including roughly $900 million of "Other Growth", $300 million of solar and storage and a $500 million Helix contribution [Q2 2026 slides, p.26].

## 5. Why customers don't leave

**Retail customers.** TXU Energy has sold power in Texas "for over 20 years" and is "the top-rated large retailer in the Texas PUC ratings" (the PUC is the state utility regulator) [10-K FY2025, Item 1] [Q2 2026 slides, p.17]. The proxy claims a "5TH CONSECUTIVE YEAR year-over-year organic growth in TXU Energy Customer Counts" without numbers [DEF 14A 2026, Performance Highlights]. The structural edge is supply from its own plants, which the company says lets it "structure products and contracts in a way that offers significant value compared to stand-alone retail electric providers" [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Sign of weakening: the annual customer count stops growing, or Retail Adjusted EBITDA falls while bad-debt expense ($201 million in 2025) rises [10-K FY2025, Item 1] [10-K FY2025, Item 8].

**Wholesale buyers.** The moat is owning plants that are hard to replace. The deck: "Owns and operates the second-largest competitive nuclear power fleet"; the CEO says data-center customers "are interested in contracting at a premium with existing, because it's still a discount to what new build would require" [Q2 2026 slides, p.12] [Q2 2026 call, l.79]. Sign of weakening: the realized-price premium over the hub (benchmark market) price in the deck's hedge table (Texas $13.09 per MWh for the balance of 2026) shrinking, or new long-term contracts stopping [Q2 2026 slides, p.19].

## 6. What could break it

Ranked by plausibility.

**1. Texas power prices stay low.** Texas plants need scarcity (hours when supply is tight and prices spike), and there has been little: "Year to date, ERCOT wholesale prices have been $30 a MWh. They were $30 a MWh last year" [Q2 2026 call, l.77]. Forward prices for 2027 are "meaningfully lower" than when the 2027 outlook was set, and the CFO now expects its lower end before acquisitions [Q2 2026 call, l.45] [Q2 2026 call, l.165]. Hedges (94% of 2027, 72% of 2028) and the PTC push the exposure to 2028 and beyond [Q2 2026 release, p.2]. Early warning: the deck's Texas forward prices falling; Texas segment Adjusted EBITDA ($1.8 billion in 2025) shrinking [Q2 2026 slides, p.20] [10-K FY2025, Item 7].

**2. Regulators change the rules.** The 10-K names "changes in market structure, and/or state or federal interference in the wholesale and retail power markets, together with subsidized generation" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Live now: PJM's cap on capacity prices for 2026–2028, proposed for two more years; a one-time "reliability backstop auction" proposed for September 2026; ERCOT's offer cap (the most a plant may bid) falling to $2,000 per MWh after prolonged scarcity [10-K FY2025, Item 1]. Early warning: PJM capacity prices clearing below the $325–333 per MW-day of the last three auctions [8-K 2026-07-14, Item 8.01]. Exposure: East Adjusted EBITDA, $2.3 billion in 2025 [10-K FY2025, Item 7].

**3. Data-center demand disappoints or contracts slip.** Risk factor: "If electricity demand does not grow at the rate expected, or if we are unable to execute on large load offtake opportunities" [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Management says a Texas audit will pause some large-load (big new power users such as data centers) connection reviews for a few months; it calls Comanche Peak "tagged towards next year" and unaffected [Q2 2026 call, l.69] [Q2 2026 call, l.109] [Q2 2026 call, l.259]. Early warning: the AWS (Q4 2027) or Meta (late 2026) start slipping in a 10-Q; ERCOT load-growth (demand-growth) wording softening again, as it did between May and August [Q1 2026 call, l.32] [Q2 2026 call, l.33]. Exposure: the growth case, including the $4.0 billion Cogentrix purchase and $19.6 billion of debt [8-K 2026-01-05, EX-99.1] [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 12].

**4. A Uri-style storm with the book short.** In February 2021 gas supply froze, Vistra's gas plants could not run, and it bought power for its retail customers "at market prices at or near the price cap": a $2.2 billion pre-tax loss [10-K FY2022, Item 7]. Winter Storm Fern (January 2026) repeated the price and collateral pattern [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. Early warning: cash margin deposits posted ($1.4 billion at June 30, 2026) jumping and liquidity (cash plus undrawn credit lines) falling from $6.3 billion [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 1] [Q2 2026 release, p.3].

**5. A plant accident.** Nuclear carries the tail risk, capped by the federal Price-Anderson scheme at $16.2 billion per incident [10-K FY2025, Note 18]. Moss Landing's battery fire (January 2025) cost about $400 million written off plus a $175 million clean-up estimate; Martin Lake's coal-unit fire (November 2024) cost about $384 million to repair [10-K FY2025, Note 8] [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 9]. Early warning: commercial availability (the share of hours a plant was ready when the market wanted it) below the low 90s, or nuclear capacity factors (output as a share of the maximum possible) below 90% outside planned refuelling outages [Q2 2026 slides, p.22].

**6. Credit and rates.** Long-term debt is $19.6 billion plus $2.5 billion of preferred stock whose dividends rank ahead of any common dividend or buyback [10-Q Q2 2026, Note 12] [10-K FY2025, Item 1A]. The plant liens (collateral pledged to lenders) released in April 2026 revert "if the applicable rating agencies withdraw the investment-grade ratings" (the safer credit-rating tiers) [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 2]. The 8% Series A preferred resets its dividend rate on October 15, 2026 to the five-year U.S. Treasury rate plus 6.93%; no decision is disclosed [10-K FY2025, Note 19]. Early warning: net debt (debt minus cash) to Adjusted EBITDA (2.6x) rising instead of heading to ~2.3x [Q2 2026 slides, p.18] [Q2 2026 slides, p.10].

**7. Environmental rules on coal.** Coal is 20% of capacity; four plants (3,558 MW, computed) retire by the end of 2027 and Miami Fort by mid-2028, and asset retirement obligations (the booked cost of closing and cleaning up plants) total $4.2 billion [10-K FY2025, Item 1] [10-K FY2025, Note 7] [10-K FY2025, Note 15]. Early warning: rules pulling dates forward, or impairment charges (write-downs of plant value).

**Concentration.** No customer is identified at or above 10% of revenue; the only disclosed concentration is credit: one unnamed counterparty was 36% of net credit exposure at June 30, 2026 [10-Q Q2 2026, Item 3].

## Glossary

- **MW, MWh, GWh, TWh:** a megawatt is a rate of power output (a large plant is about 1,000 MW); a megawatt-hour is that rate for one hour, the unit power is priced in; a gigawatt-hour is a thousand MWh and a terawatt-hour a million.
- **ERCOT:** the grid operator and wholesale market for most of Texas, which pays plants only for energy sold, not for being available.
- **PJM:** the grid operator and wholesale market for much of the mid-Atlantic and Midwest, with both an energy market and a capacity auction.
- **PPA (power purchase agreement):** a long-term contract in which one buyer takes a plant's output on agreed terms, removing it from the open market.
- **Capacity auction:** an auction held years ahead in which a grid operator pays plants a daily fee per megawatt for promising to be available in a future delivery year.
- **Nuclear production tax credit (PTC):** a federal credit of up to $15 per MWh for existing nuclear plants (2024–2032) that shrinks as the plant's own revenue per MWh rises.
- **Market heat rate / spark spread:** the power price divided by the gas price (heat rate), or power price minus the gas cost of making it (spark spread): a gas plant's margin and the signal of scarcity.
- **Mark-to-market (unrealized gain or loss):** the quarterly revaluation of hedge contracts against current forward prices, booked in revenue although no cash changes hands until delivery.
- **Adjusted EBITDA:** the company's profit measure: net income before interest, tax and depreciation, with unrealized hedge swings and one-off items removed.
- **Adjusted FCFbG (Adjusted Free Cash Flow before Growth):** the company's cash measure: operating cash flow excluding hedge collateral and working-capital moves, minus maintenance capex, before growth capex.
